\begin{abstract}
  The square of a graph is obtained by joining every pair of vertices whose distance is at most two. Given a graph $G$, the graph square root problem asks whether there exists a graph $H$ such that $G=H^2$. Whilst this problem admits polynomial-time algorithms for restricted classes of root graphs, the general decision problem is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete.

  In this thesis, rather than studying the construction problem directly and using reduction techniques to ascertain polynomial-time algorithms, we formulate the problem as an integer program based on the relationship between the adjacency matrices of a root $H$ and its square $G$. By constructing the corresponding linear relaxation, the problem can then be situated within polynomial time through the use of interior-point methods.

  The seminal result for the problem is the work by \citeauthor{ross_1960_square}, who approached the problem by restricting the root graphs to trees. Through analyzing the number of cliques which each vertex in the graph square is situated in, they gave an algorithm for finding the unique tree root up to isomorphism. In contrast, our formulation provides an optimization-based approach that can be iteratively strengthened through the addition of further constraints. These constraints refine the linear relaxation, enabling tree roots to be represented by integral solutions to the linear program, while graphs with no possible roots are identified through fractional or infeasible solutions. Rather than relying purely on heuristics, our approach exploits structural properties of the graph square and its root to obtain an exact solution. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Background} \label{sec::background}

In 1949, Leon Festinger proposed a novel method in the field of social psychology for analyzing relationships among a number of people present \cite{festinger_bahoken_beauguitte_2023}. Classically, this was represented using a sociogram, a visual tool that maps out the relationships between people. However, Forsyth and Katz introduced the use of a matrix to represent the sociometric pattern, with rows and columns corresponding to individuals, $1$s for choices, and blank diagonals. Festinger noticed that his structure, reminiscent of the adjacency matrix defined by \citeauthor{konig_1936} in \cite{konig_1936}, also had the remarkable property that the action of squaring a matrix 
\[
A_{rc}^2 = A_{1c}A_{r1} + \dots + A_{nc}A_{rn} \footnote{$A_{rc}^2$ is more naturally written as $\displaystyle \sum_{k = 1}^n A_{ck}A_{kr}$ using the commutativity of multiplication $A_{1c}A_{r1} = A_{c1}A_{1r}$, which intuitively gives the notion of paths from c to r via intermediary k}
\]
represented the number of ``two-step'' connections. This same approach can then be used to denote ``three-step'' connections between any two people  
\[
A_{rc}^3 = A_{1c}A_{r1}^2 + \dots + A_{nc}A_{rn}^2, \footnote{$A_{rc}^3$ is more naturally written as $\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n A_{ck}A^2_{kr}$, which intuitively counts the number of paths from $c$ to $r$ that go through an intermediate vertex $k$, followed by any two-step path from $k$ to $r$.}
\]
Higher steps can be easily generated in the same way. In this manner, higher powers of the adjacency matrix encode the number of walks of increasing length between pairs of individuals. For the two-step case, this allows densely connected groups of people, known as cliques, to be identified.

In the process of improving Festinger's clique detection methods, \citeauthor{ross_1960_square} showed that complete graphs may be realized as squares of stars by considering powers of such matrices \cite{ross_1960_square}. They introduced a structural characterization of the square of a tree in terms of clique intersections, ``multicliqual'' vertices, and so called ``neighborly'' properties, proving that a graph is the square of a tree if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
  \item every vertex is every vertex is adjacent to at least one other vertex,
  \item no two maximal cliques intersect in more than two vertices, and
  \item the number of maximal clique pairs intersecting in two vertices is exactly one less than the total number of maximal cliques. 
\end{enumerate}

Using these properties, they constructed an explicit algorithm that recovers the underlying tree for a given graph square. Their work has become the foundational reference for the study of graph squares and is universally cited in subsequent literature on the subject, and will be explored in more detail later. 

Since then, the problem of finding the square root of a graph has extended beyond sociology and is a well-studied field in both mathematics and computer-science, with direct applications in distributed computing (e.g., see \cite{peres_2023_distributed}), network design and computational biology (e.g., see \cite{zhou_2025_biology}). This is in part because many structural properties of graph roots are first established in the square case before being extended to the more general $k$-th power setting (e.g., see \cite{harary_1962_planarity}). 

Research in this area has primarily focused around an undirected graph $H$ and its square $G = H^2$, which is obtained by adding an edge between every pair of vertices whose distance in $H$ is at most two. This two-step undirected graph square has been studied extensively with much of the focus being on the complexity of the decision problem - given a specific graph square $G$ does there exist a root graph $H$ such that $H^2=G$. \citeauthor{motwani_sudan_1994_roots} showed that deciding if a graph admits a root is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete. Subsequent studies in the area have restricted the decision problem in two ways, either by considering various properties on the root graph or by considering properties on the graph square. 

\section{Literature} \label{sec::literature}

Focusing first on special undirected graph classes, the aforementioned work by \citeauthor{ross_1960_square} in 1960 gives an algorithm for constructing a tree root by using characterizations of the graph square. As part of this work they proved tree square roots are unique up to isomorphism. This was the first major contribution to the study of graph roots and provided fundamental structural characteristics of graph squares, laying the groundwork for later research on graph root problems and their applications. Building upon these insights, \citeauthor{lin_1995_square_roots} bypassed the exponential bottleneck of general maximal clique enumeration present in Ross and Harary's approach, by developing an optimal $\mathcal{O}(\vert{}V\vert{} + \vert{}E\vert{})$ linear time algorithm to recognize tree squares and reconstruct their unique tree roots\cite{lin_1995_square_roots}.

\citeauthor{fleischner_1974_square} in 1974 showed that as long as a graph is 2-connected its graph square will have a Hamiltonian cycle. This established a broad condition guaranteeing Hamiltonicity in graph squares which is used in many subsequent works such as \cite{brandt_muttel_rautenbach_2014}. 

\citeauthor{lau_corneil_2004_chordal_root} in 2004 provided an algorithm for constructing square roots from an interval square in polynomial time, and showed that the decision problem for finding roots of chordal graphs, roots of split graphs, and chordal roots of graphs are $\mathbf{NP}$-complete \cite{lau_corneil_2004_chordal_root}. Furthermore, Lau gave a linear-time algorithm for constructing square roots of bipartite graphs, and proved that deciding whether a bipartite graph has a cube root is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete \cite{lau_2006_bipartite_roots}. \citeauthor{le_tuy_2007_block_square} in 2007 generalized tree-root results further by giving a linear time recognition of roots of block graphs \cite{le_tuy_2007_block_square}. 

Generalizing the notion of graph squares, in 1967 \citeauthor{mukhopadhyay_1967_root} provided necessary and sufficient conditions for a connected graph to have a square root \cite{mukhopadhyay_1967_root}, whilst \citeauthor{geller_1970_root} in 1970 likewise found necessary and sufficient conditions for a digraph to have a square root \cite{geller_1970_root}. 

In 1994 \citeauthor{motwani_sudan_1994_roots} established the complexity of the square problem for the generalized undirected case and showed that the problem of determining if a given graph has a square root is an $\mathbf{NP}$-complete problem via a polynomial reduction to $3$-CNF not-all-equal SAT \cite{motwani_sudan_1994_roots}.

Their work led to joint work by Farzad, Le, Tuy, Lau and Karimi in 2009 and 2012, in which they established a partial dichotomy theorem for the complexity of determining if a given undirected graph of girth $g$ has a square root \cite{farzad_2009_roots} which was informally completed under \cite{farzad_karimi_2012_root}. In 2013, \citeauthor{cochefert_et_al_2013_sparse_square_roots} gave an algorithm for constructing a square root with minimal number of edges from undirected graphs with max degree $\leq 6$ in polynomial time and an algorithm for constructing maximal edge square roots in exponential time \cite{cochefert_et_al_2013_sparse_square_roots}. 

In recent years, Duffy provided a full classification for the undirected square of oriented graphs satisfying max degree $\leq 3$ and girth $\geq 4$ \cite{duffy_2025_oriented_root}. 

\subsection{Overview} \label{subsec::overview}

In addition to providing a general overview of the research direction in this section we first standardise the definitions that will be used for the rest of this thesis. We then introduce fundamental theory on the three areas of: complexity theory, graph theory and mathematical optimization, which are used in the various algorithmic approaches to the decision problem. 

The first construction we examine is the reduction used by \citeauthor{motwani_sudan_1994_roots} in showing that the decision problem determining whether a given graph $G$ admits a square root is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete \cite{motwani_sudan_1994_roots}. Next, we introduce the polynomial time algorithm given by \citeauthor{ross_1960_square} for constructing a tree root from a given square in detail. Finally, we formulate the problem as an integer program. 

In Chapter $3$ we present the ideas of linear relaxation and discuss the results of each program and their validity. To obtain these results, we conducted an exhaustive enumeration on all non-isomorphic connected graphs up to $n=8$ vertices in addition to obtaining theoretical results. For each such graph, we formulated the corresponding linear program and solved it using a standard LP solver using interior point method. The outcomes of these computations are categorized as integral, fractional, or infeasible and are analyzed. 

Finally, we introduce several augmentations to the linear program, consisting of constraints that encode structural bounds on both the graph square and its underlying root. In particular, we show that by adding specific constraints to the LP, we can ensure that the square of all trees will return a feasible integer solution. This augmentation ensures that the LP returns a tree root in polynomial time, whilst ensuring that all squares with roots remain LP feasible. 

\section{Definitions and Notations} \label{sec::definitions_and_notations}

\subsection{Complexity Theory} \label{subsec::complexity_theory}

Complexity theory studies the classification of computational problems and explores the amount of resources required to solve these problems. Complexity theory considers both time and space as critical resources and provides a standardized approach across various different problems. 

\begin{definition}[Big-$O$ Notation] \label{def::big_O_notation}
  Let $f,g:\mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. The set $O(g(n))$ is defined as
  \[
  O(g(n)) = \left\{ f \mid \text{ there exists } c > 0, n_0 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } f(n) \leq c g(n) \text{ for all } n \geq n_0 \right\}.
  \]
  We write $f(n)=O(g(n))$ as shorthand for $f(n)\in O(g(n))$.
\end{definition}

Using this notation, we can classify algorithms based on their runtime. 

\begin{definition}[Polynomial-Time Algorithm] \label{def::polynomial_time_algorithm}
  An algorithm is said to run in \textbf{polynomial time} if there exists a constant $c \ge 0$ such that its run time on inputs of size $n$ is $O(n^c)$.
\end{definition}

Polynomial-time algorithms are important because they are generally regarded as efficient and computationally ``simple''. Consequently, the class of problems that admit polynomial-time algorithms forms the basis for the complexity class $\mathbf{P}$. 

\begin{definition}[Decision Problem] \label{def::decision_problem}
  A \textbf{decision problem} is a computational problem whose output is either \textbf{Yes} or \textbf{No} for every valid input instance. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Class $\mathbf{P}$] \label{def::class_p}
  The complexity class $\mathbf{P}$ consists of all decision problems that can be solved by a deterministic algorithm in polynomial time.
\end{definition}

Unfortunately many important decision problems are not known to admit such polynomial-time algorithms and hence need additional classification on their ``hardness''. For example, determining whether a graph is $3$-colourable is a well known problem for which no polynomial-time algorithm is known, despite the fact that a proposed $3$-colouring can be checked efficiently. This motivates the study of complexity classes based not only on how efficiently a solution can be found, but also on how a proposed solution can be verified efficiently.

\begin{definition}[Certificate] \label{def::certificate}
  Let $L$ be a decision problem. A \textbf{certificate} for an instance $x$ of $L$ is a finite string $c$ that provides evidence that $x$ is a \textbf{Yes}-instance of $L$. A certificate is said to be \textbf{polynomially verifiable} if there exists a deterministic algorithm that for given input $(x,c)$, can determine whether $c$ is a valid certificate for $x$ in time polynomial in $|x|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Class $\mathbf{NP}$] \label{def::class_np}
  The complexity class $\mathbf{NP}$ consists of all decision problems for which every \textbf{Yes}-instance admits a certificate that is polynomially verifiable.
\end{definition}

Since every decision problem in $\mathbf{P}$ can be solved in polynomial time, a solution can also be verified in polynomial time,
\[
\mathbf{P} \subseteq \mathbf{NP}.
\]
Whether this inclusion is strict remains one of the most important open questions in theoretical computer science - the $\mathbf{P}$ vs $\mathbf{NP}$ problem. Reductions provide a means of comparing the relative computational difficulty of problems in $\mathbf{NP}$ by establishing whether one problem is at least as hard as another. In particular, if $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}$, then every problem in $\mathbf{NP}$ is solvable in polynomial time.

\begin{definition}[Polynomial-Time Reduction] \label{def::polynomial_time_reduction}
  Let $A$ and $B$ be decision problems, with sets of instances
  $\mathcal{I}_A$ and $\mathcal{I}_B$ and sets of \textbf{Yes}-instances $Y_A \subseteq \mathcal{I}_A$ and $Y_B \subseteq \mathcal{I}_B$. A \textbf{polynomial-time reduction} from $A$ to $B$, denoted $A \leq_p B$, is a polynomial-time computable function $f \colon \mathcal{I}_A \rightarrow \mathcal{I}_B$ such that, for every instance $x \in \mathcal{I}_A$,
  \[
  x \in Y_A \quad \text{if and only if} \quad f(x) \in Y_B.
  \]
\end{definition}

This notion of reduction allows the computational difficulty of problems to be compared. In particular, a problem is $\mathbf{NP}$-hard if every problem in $\mathbf{NP}$ can be reduced to it via a polynomial time reduction.

\begin{definition}[Class $\mathbf{NP}$-Hard] \label{def::class_np_hard}
  A decision problem $B$ is \textbf{$\mathbf{NP}$-hard} if every problem $A \in \mathbf{NP}$ satisfies $A \leq_p B$. The collection of all $\mathbf{NP}$-hard problems is denoted by the class \textbf{$\mathbf{NP}$-hard}.
\end{definition}

Importantly, an $\mathbf{NP}$-hard problem many not necessary lie in $\mathbf{NP}$. If it does, it is called $\mathbf{NP}$-complete. 

\begin{definition}[Class $\mathbf{NP}$-Complete] \label{def::class_np_complete}
  A decision problem $B$ is \textbf{$\mathbf{NP}$-complete} if $B \in \mathbf{NP}$, and $B$ is $\mathbf{NP}$-hard. The collection of all $\mathbf{NP}$-complete problems is denoted by the class \textbf{$\mathbf{NP}$-complete}.
\end{definition}

One can establish that a problem is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete by showing that it belongs to $\mathbf{NP}$ and that a known $\mathbf{NP}$-complete problem can be reduced to it in polynomial time. 

The first known $\mathbf{NP}$-complete problem was established by the Cook-Levin theorem and is the Boolean satisfiability problem, or SAT. 

\begin{definition}[Boolean Satisfiability Problem] \label{def::SAT}
  The \textbf{Boolean satisfiability problem} (\textbf{SAT}) asks whether there exists an assignment of truth values to the variables of a given Boolean formula such that the formula evaluates to $\textsc{True}$. A Boolean formula is said to be \textbf{satisfiable} if such an assignment exists.
\end{definition}

Any Boolean formula can be transformed into an equisatisfiable conjunctive normal form (CNF) formula of linear size by introducing additional variables using the \textbf{Tseitin transformation} \cite{tseitin_1968_transformation}. This transformation preserves satisfiability while ensuring only linear growth in the size of the resulting conjunctive normal form Boolean formula.  

\begin{definition}[Conjunctive Normal Form]
\label{def::CNF}
  A Boolean formula is in \textbf{conjunctive normal form} (\textbf{CNF}) if it is expressed as a conjunction of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction of literals. That is, a CNF formula has the form
  \[
  C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m, \quad \text {where each clause $C_i$ is of the form} \quad C_i = \ell_{i1} \lor \ell_{i2} \lor \cdots \lor \ell_{ik_i}
  \]
  and each $\ell_{ij}$ is either a Boolean variable or its complement, denoted with $\overline{\ell_{ij}}$.
\end{definition}

A further restriction is obtained by requiring each clause in a CNF formula to contain exactly three literals. This can be done using $3$-CNF transformation and gives the following problem.

\begin{definition}[$3$-SAT] \label{def::3SAT}
  The \textbf{$3$-SAT} problem asks whether there exists an assignment of truth values to the variables of a Boolean formula in conjunctive normal form such that every clause contains exactly three literals and the formula evaluates to $\textsc{True}$. It is formally structured as
  \leavevmode

  \textbf{Instance:} A Boolean formula $\varphi$ in conjunctive normal form in which each clause contains exactly three literals.
  \medskip

  \textbf{Question:} Does there exist an assignment of truth values to the variables of $\varphi$ such that every clause is satisfied?
\end{definition}

The decision problem \textbf{$3$-SAT} is a well-known $\mathbf{NP}$-complete problem. It is commonly used as a starting point for proving the $\mathbf{NP}$-completeness of other decision problems. In particular, we will reduce $3$-SAT to the \textbf{SQUARE} \footnote{The \textbf{SQUARE} problem is defined in
Definition~\ref{prob::square_problem}.} problem to establish that the latter is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete.

\subsection{Graph Theory} \label{subsec::graph_theory}

Informally, a graph is relational structure that captures relationships between discrete objects. 

\begin{definition}[Graph] \label{def::graph}
  A \textbf{graph} $G$ is an ordered pair $(V,E)$, where $V$ is a set of vertices and $E$ is a set of unordered pairs of distinct vertices in $V$. Each element of $E$ is called an \textbf{edge} of $G$.
\end{definition}

The number of vertices and edges in a graph provide basic measures of its order and size.

\begin{definition}[Order and Size] \label{def::order_size}
  The \textbf{order} of a graph $G$, denoted by $|G|$, is the number of vertices in $G$, while the \textbf{size} of $G$ is the number of edges. Thus, if $V_G=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ and $E_G=\{e_1,\ldots,e_m\}$, then $|G|=n$ and $|E_G|=m$.
\end{definition}

For a vertex $v$, the number of edges incident to it is its degree.

\begin{definition}[Degree] \label{def::degree}
  The \textbf{degree} of a vertex $v$ in a graph $G$, denoted by $\deg_G(v)$, is the number of edges incident to $v$. The minimum and maximum degrees of $G$ are denoted by
  \[
  \delta(G)=\min_{v\in V_G}\deg_G(v) \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta(G)=\max_{v\in V_G}\deg_G(v),
  \]
  respectively.
\end{definition}

The degree of a vertex can also be described through the vertices to which it is adjacent to.

\begin{definition}[Neighbourhood] \label{def::neighbourhood}
  For a vertex $v\in V_G$, the \textbf{open neighbourhood} of $v$ denoted by $N_G(v)$, is the set of vertices adjacent to $v$ in $G$. The \textbf{closed neighbourhood} of $v$ includes vertex $v$ and is denoted by 
  \[
  N_G[v]=N_G(v)\cup\{v\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

Connections between vertices in a graph can be described using walks, paths and cycles.

\begin{definition}[Walk] \label{def::walk}
  A \textbf{walk} from a vertex $v_i$ to a vertex $v_j$ in a graph $G$ is an alternating sequence of vertices and edges
  \[
  W=\langle v_i,e_1,v_1,e_2,\ldots,e_k,v_j\rangle,
  \]
  where each edge $e_r$ has endpoints given by the vertices immediately preceding and following it in the sequence. The number of edges in $W$ is called the \textbf{length} of the walk.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Path] \label{def::path}
  A \textbf{path} is a walk in which no vertex is repeated. A path containing $p$ vertices is denoted by $P_p$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cycle] \label{def::cycle}
  A \textbf{cycle} is a closed path in which the first and last vertices are the same and all other vertices are distinct. A cycle containing $c$ vertices is denoted by $C_c$. 
\end{definition}

The length of a shortest cycle is an important structural parameter of a graph.

\begin{definition}[Girth] \label{def::girth}
  The \textbf{girth} of a graph $G$, denoted by $g(G)$, is the length of the shortest cycle in $G$. If $G$ contains no cycle, its girth is $\infty$.
\end{definition}

A finite connected graph with infinite girth is called a tree.

\begin{definition}[Tree] \label{def::tree}
  A \textbf{tree} is a connected graph that contains no cycles.
\end{definition}

The vertices of a tree can be classified according to their degree. 

\begin{definition}[Leaf] \label{def::leaf}
  A \textbf{leaf} of a tree $T$ is a vertex of degree one.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Stem] \label{def::stem}
  A \textbf{stem} of a tree $T$ is the vertex adjacent to a leaf of
  $T$.
\end{definition}

The length of a shortest path provides a natural measure of the distance between two vertices.

\begin{definition}[Distance] \label{def::distance}
  The \textbf{distance} between two vertices $u$ and $v$ on the graph $G$, denoted by $d_G(u,v)$, is the number of edges of the shortest path between vertices $u$ and $v$ in $G$. If no such path exists, $d_G(u,v) = \infty$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Diameter] \label{def::diameter}
  The \textbf{diameter} of a graph $G$ is
  \[
  \operatorname{diam}(G) = \max_{u,v\in V_G}d_G(u,v).
  \]
  For a disconnected graph, the diameter is $\infty$.
\end{definition}

If a graph has finite diameter it is connected. 

\begin{definition}[Connected Graph] \label{def::connected_graph}
  A graph $G$ is \textbf{connected} if there exists a path between every pair of vertices in $G$ Otherwise, $G$ is \textbf{disconnected}.
\end{definition}

A graph can be further categorized according to whether multiple edges between a vertex and itself are permitted, whether there are infinite number of vertices and edges, whether the edges represent an unordered or ordered relationship between their endpoints, and whether the vertices are labelled or unlabelled. 

\begin{definition}[Simple Graph] \label{def::simple_graph}
  A \textbf{simple graph} $G=(V,E)$ is a graph in which no two distinct edges connect the same pair of vertices and no edge connects a vertex to itself. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Finite Graph] \label{def::finite_graph}
  A \textbf{finite graph} is a graph $G=(V,E)$ for which both $V$ and $E$ are finite sets. A graph that is not finite is called an \textbf{infinite graph}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed Graph] \label{def::directed_graph}
  A \textbf{directed graph} $G=(V,E)$ is a graph in which each edge $e\in E$ is an ordered pair of distinct vertices in $V$. Thus, $(u,v)$ represents an edge directed from $u$ to $v$, where 
  $(u,v)$ may not necessarily be equal to $(v,u)$.

  An \textbf{undirected graph} $G=(V,E)$ is a graph in which each edge $e\in E$ is an unordered pair of distinct vertices in $V$. Thus, $\{u,v\}=\{v,u\}$ for every edge between vertices $u$ and $v$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Labelled Graph] \label{def::labelled_graph}
  A \textbf{labelled graph} is a graph $G=(V,E)$ together with a labelling function $\ell:V\rightarrow L$, where $L$ is a set of labels. A graph is \textbf{unlabelled} when no such vertex labels are specified. 
\end{definition}

In this thesis, we exclusively study finite, simple, undirected, unlabelled graphs. However, the constructions and algorithms may use additional structure, including labels or multiple edges, which must be analyzed and restricted to their corresponding simple, unlabelled counterparts.

In particular, two graphs may have different vertex sets while nevertheless having the same underlying structure. This motivates the following notion of isomorphism.

\begin{definition}[Graph Isomorphism] \label{def::graph_isomorphism}
  Let $G$ and $H$ be graphs. An \textbf{isomorphism} from $G$ to $H$ is a bijection
  \[
  f:V_G\rightarrow V_H \quad \text{such that} \quad uv\in E_G \text{ if and only if } f(u)f(v)\in E_H.
  \]
  We say that $G$ is \textbf{isomorphic} to $H$, written $G\cong H$, if there exists an isomorphism from $G$ to $H$.
\end{definition}

The isomorphism relation is an equivalence relation on the set of graphs, and an \textbf{isomorphism class} is an equivalence class of graphs under this relation. Thus, graphs within the same isomorphism class have the same structure up to a relabelling of their vertices.

We will also consider graphs obtained by restricting the vertices and edges of another graph.

\begin{definition}[Subgraph] \label{def::subgraph}
  A graph $H=(V_H,E_H)$ is a \textbf{subgraph} of a graph $G=(V_G,E_G)$ if
  \[
  V_H\subseteq V_G \quad \text{and} \quad E_H\subseteq E_G.
  \]
  For $S\subseteq V_G$, the \textbf{induced subgraph} of $G$ on $S$, denoted by $G[S]$, is the graph with vertex set $S$ and edge set
  \[
  E_{G[S]}=\{uv\in E_G:u,v\in S\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

One of the most important types of graphs is the complete graph, and its subgraph counterpart. 

\begin{definition}[Complete Graph] \label{def::complete_graph}
  A graph on $n$ vertices is \textbf{complete} if every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent. The complete graph on $n$ vertices is denoted by $K_n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Clique] \label{def::clique}
  A \textbf{clique} of a graph $G$ is a subset $S\subseteq V_G$ such that the induced subgraph $G[S]$ is complete. The \textbf{clique number} of $G$, denoted by $\omega(G)$, is the size of a largest clique in $G$. A clique containing $k$ vertices is denoted by $K_k$. 
\end{definition}

It is important to distinguish maximal cliques from cliques in general since any vertex belonging to a clique of size $n$ naturally belongs to a clique of size $n-1$.

\begin{definition} \label{def::multicliqual_vertex}
  A \textbf{multicliqual vertex} is a vertex that belongs to multiple maximal cliques. A \textbf{unicliqual vertex} is a vertex that belongs to only one maximal clique.    
\end{definition}

In addition to being specified using a set of vertices and a set of edges, a graph can also be represented using a matrix.

\begin{definition}[Adjacency Matrix] \label{def::adjacency_matrix}
  The \textbf{adjacency matrix} of graph $H$ is denoted by $A_H \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ where
  \[
  (A_H)_{ij} =
  \begin{cases}
  1 ,& \text{if } ij \in E_H, \\
  0 ,& \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \]
\end{definition}

The degrees of the vertices can similarly be represented by a diagonal matrix.

\begin{definition}[Degree Matrix] \label{def::degree_matrix}
  The \textbf{degree matrix} of graph $H$ is denoted by $D_H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ where
  \[
  (D_H)_{ij} =
  \begin{cases}
  \deg_H(i) ,& \text{if } i = j, \\
  0 ,& \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \]
\end{definition}

In our discussion of graphs represented as matrices, we will find the following notation useful. 

\begin{definition}[Indicator Function] \label{def::indicator_function}
  Let $\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1} : \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \to \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ denote the entrywise \textbf{indicator function}, defined by
  \[
  \mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}(M)_{ij} =
  \begin{cases}
  1 ,& \text{if } M_{ij} \geq 1, \\
  0 ,& \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \]
\end{definition}

We now introduce the graph-theoretic objects central to this thesis.

\begin{definition}[Square of a Graph, Root] \label{def::square_of_a_graph}
  Let the \textbf{graph root} $H = (V, E_H)$ be a finite, simple, undirected graph, where $V$ is the vertex set with $|V| = n$, and $E_H \subseteq \{\{u,v\} \mid u,v \in V, u \neq v\}$ is the edge set.  
  The \textbf{square of a graph} $H$, denoted by $H^2 = (V, E_{H^2})$ is the graph on the same vertex set with edge set
  \begin{equation} \label{eq::fund_def_square}
    E_{H^2} = \{\, uv \mid u \neq v, 1 \leq d_H(u,v) \leq 2 \,\}.
  \end{equation}
  That is, two distinct vertices are adjacent in $H^2$ if and only if they are connected by a path of length one or two in $H$. When $G = H^2$ we say that $H$ is a \textbf{root} of $G$ 
\end{definition}

Equivalently, $G$ is a root of $H$ when the adjacency matrices of $G$ and $H$ satisfy  
\begin{equation} \label{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected}
  A_G = \mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\!\big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big).
\end{equation}

We prove this equivalence in Chapter 2.

\subsection{Mathematical Optimization} \label{subsec::mathematical_optimization}

Mathematical optimization studies the problem of finding the best solution from all feasible solutions which are defined by a collection of constraints. An optimization problem usually consists of decision variables, an objective function to be maximized or minimized, and a set of constraints restricting the feasible values of the variables.

\begin{definition}[Integer Program] \label{def::integer_program}
  An \textbf{integer program} ($IP$) is an optimization problem of the form
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{max/min}\quad & c^\top x\\
    \text{subject to}\quad & Ax\leq b,\\
    & x\in\mathbb{Z}^n,
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  where $x\in\mathbb{Z}^n$ is the vector of decision variables, $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the objective coefficient vector, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is the constraint matrix, and $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$ is the constraint vector.
\end{definition}

A linear relaxation is obtained by removing the integrality requirements from an integer program while retaining its linear objective function and constraints.

\begin{definition}[Linear Relaxation] \label{def::linear_relaxation}
  Let $IP$ be an integer program. The \textbf{linear relaxation} of $IP$ is the linear program obtained by removing the requirement that its variables take integer values.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Linear Program] \label{def::linear_program}
  A \textbf{linear program} ($LP$) is an optimization problem of the form
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{max/min}\quad & c^\top x\\
  \text{subject to}\quad & Ax\leq b,\\
  & x\in\mathbb{R}^n,
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  where $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the vector of decision variables, $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the objective coefficient vector, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is the constraint matrix, and $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$ is the constraint vector.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Feasible Solution] \label{def::feasible_solution}
  A \textbf{feasible solution} to an optimization problem is an assignment of values to its decision variables that satisfies all of its constraints. The set of all feasible solutions is called the \textbf{feasible set} and is denoted by $\mathcal{F}$. Geometrically, $\mathcal{F}$ is the subset of the decision-variable space satisfying all of the constraints and is referred to as the \textbf{feasible region}.
\end{definition}

Among the feasible solutions, an optimal solution is one that achieves the optimum value of the objective function.

\begin{definition}[Optimal Solution] \label{def::optimal_solution}
  An \textbf{optimal solution} is a feasible solution that maximizes or minimizes the objective function, depending on the optimization problem. The corresponding value of the objective function is called the \textbf{optimal value} and is denoted by $\operatorname{OPT}$.
\end{definition}

When an integer program is relaxed to a linear program, every feasible integer solution remains feasible for the relaxation. Consequently, if $\mathcal{F}_{IP}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{LP}$ denote the feasible sets of the integer program and its linear relaxation respectively, then
\[
\mathcal{F}_{IP} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{LP}.
\]
Geometrically, $\mathcal{F}_{IP}$ is represented by the feasible integer points in the decision-variable space, whereas $\mathcal{F}_{LP}$ is the polyhedron defined by the relaxed constraints and may contain additional points.

\input{subfiles/LinearRelaxationSetGeometry.tex}

For a maximization problem, this set inclusion implies $\operatorname{OPT}_{IP} \leq \operatorname{OPT}_{LP}$. Therefore  the the optimal value for the linear relaxation provides an upper bound on the optimal value of the integer program. For a minimization problem, $\operatorname{OPT}_{LP} \leq \operatorname{OPT}_{IP}$, so the linear relaxation provides a lower bound.

\begin{definition}[Integer Hull] \label{def::integer_hull}
  Let $\mathcal{F}_{IP}$ denote the feasible set of an integer program. The \textbf{integer hull} is the convex hull of the feasible integer solutions, denoted by $\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{F}_{IP})$. That is, the integer hull is the set of all convex combinations of points in $\mathcal{F}_{IP}$.
\end{definition}

Since the feasible region of a linear program is convex, the integer hull is contained in the feasible region of its linear relaxation. Hence,
\[
\mathcal{F}_{IP} \subseteq \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{F}_{IP}) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{LP}.
\]
The integer hull is the smallest convex set containing all feasible integer solutions. Thus, geometrically, the gap between the integer hull and the LP feasible region represents the additional solutions admitted by the relaxation. If
\[
\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{F}_{IP}) = \mathcal{F}_{LP},
\]
then the linear relaxation coincides with the integer hull and is therefore an exact convex description of the feasible integer solutions.


\chapter{Algorithmic Approaches} \label{chap::algorithmic_approaches}

In this chapter we explore different methodologies that have been used to show certain properties of the graph root and the graph square. For many restricted cases which are $\mathbf{NP}$, a polynomial time reduction is given and in cases which are polynomial, a polynomial time algorithm is given.  

\section{NP-Completeness Proof} \label{sec::np_completeness_proof}

We now establish the computational complexity of the graph square problem as was done by \citeauthor{motwani_sudan_1994_roots} in their paper \cite{motwani_sudan_1994_roots}. The decision version of the problem asks whether a given graph admits a root.

\begin{problem}[SQUARE] \label{prob::square_problem}
  \leavevmode

  \textbf{Instance:} A finite, simple, undirected graph $G=(V,E)$.
  \medskip

  \textbf{Question:} Does there exist a finite, simple, undirected graph $H$ such that
  \[
  G = H^2?
  \]
\end{problem}

\begin{theorem}[$\mathbf{NP}$] \label{theorem::np}
  The decision problem \textbf{SQUARE} is in $\mathbf{NP}$. 
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[NP Proof]
  Given a candidate square root $H$ on $n$ vertices, it is sufficient to verify that $G=H^2$. For each pair of distinct vertices $u,v\in V_H$, check whether $u$ and $v$ are adjacent in $H$ or have a common neighbour in $H$. This determines whether $d_H(u,v) \leq 2$ and therefore whether $uv \in E_{H^2}$.

  There are $\displaystyle \binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ unordered pairs of vertices. For each pair, determining whether the vertices have a common neighbour requires at most $O(n)$ operations. Hence, the total verification time is
  \[
  O(n^2) \cdot O(n) = O(n^3).
  \]
  By the definition of Big-$O$ notation, this is polynomial in $n$. Therefore, the certificate $H$ can be verified in polynomial time, and consequently
  \[
  \text{SQUARE} \in \mathbf{NP}.
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{problem}[NAESAT] \label{prob::naesat_problem}
\leavevmode

\textbf{Instance:} A $3$-CNF formula
\[
\phi = C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m
\]
where each clause $C_i$ is over variables $x_1,\ldots,x_n$.

\medskip

\textbf{Question:} Does there exist an assignment of $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$ to the variables such that every clause contains at least one $\textsc{True}$ literal and at least one $\textsc{False}$ literal?
\end{problem}

Thus, an assignment satisfies the not-all-equal condition if, for every clause, its literals are not all assigned the same truth value. The problem NAESAT is known to be $\mathbf{NP}$-complete by Schaefer's dichotomy theorem \cite{schaefer_1978_complexity}. 

\subsection{NP-Hard proof} \label{subsec::np_hard_proof}

Following \cite{motwani_sudan_1994_roots}, we show \textbf{SQUARE} is $\mathbf{NP}$-hard by giving a polynomial-time reduction from $\mathbf{NP}$-complete NAESAT and then show that $G_\phi$ has a root if and only if $\phi \in \mathrm{NAESAT}$. 

\begin{proof}[Polynomial Reduction] \label{proof::polynomial_reduction}
  Let $\phi = C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m $ be an instance of NAESAT, where $\phi$ is a $3$-CNF formula on variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$. Over variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$, construct a graph $G_\phi = (V_\phi,E_\phi)$ such that
  \[
  \phi \in \textsc{NAESAT} \quad \text{if and only if} \quad G_\phi \text{ has a square root}.
  \]
  The vertex set $V_\phi$ consists of
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Two constant vertices $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$, plus a vertex $x$ and three tail vertices $t_1, t_2, t_3$ for $x$.
    \item For each of the $2n$ literals $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_{2n}$, where $\ell_{i+n} = \overline{\ell_i}$, a literal vertex $L_i$. 
    \item For every pair of literals $\ell_i,\ell_k$ that are not complements of each other, a literal-pair vertex $A_{ik}$.
    \item For each clause $C_j=\{\ell_{j_1},\ell_{j_2},\ell_{j_3}\}$, a clause vertex $C_j$ together with three tail vertices $C_j^1,C_j^2,C_j^3$.
  \end{enumerate}

  The edge set $E_\phi$ defined as
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Tail of $x$.} The tail of $x$ consists of the edges
    \[
    t_3t_2,\quad t_3t_1,\quad t_2t_1,\quad t_2x,\quad t_1x,\quad t_1\text{$\textsc{True}$},\quad t_1\text{$\textsc{False}$}.
    \]
    \item \textbf{Tails of the clause vertices.} For every clause $C_j$, introduce the edges
    \[
    C_j^3C_j^2,\quad C_j^3C_j^1,\quad C_j^2C_j^1,\quad C_j^2C_j,\quad C_j^1C_j, \quad C_j^1L_{j_1},\quad C_j^1L_{j_2},\quad C_j^1L_{j_3},
    \]
    where $C_j=\{\ell_{j_1},\ell_{j_2},\ell_{j_3}\}$.
    \item \textbf{Edges incident to clause vertices.} For every clause $C_j$ and every literal $\ell_i \in C_j$, introduce the edge $C_jL_i$. Also introduce $C_j\text{$\textsc{True}$}$ and $C_j\text{$\textsc{False}$}$. Finally, for every literal $\ell_k$ that is not the complement of a literal in $C_j$, introduce the edge $C_jA_{ik}$.
    \item \textbf{Edges among the remaining vertices.} All vertices in
    \[
    \{L_i:1\leq i\leq 2n\} \cup \{A_{ik}: \ell_i \neq \overline{\ell_k}\} \cup \{\text{$\textsc{True}$},\text{$\textsc{False}$},x\}
    \]
    are adjacent to one another, except for pairs of complementary literals. Thus, $L_iL_k\in E_\phi$ whenever $\ell_i$ and $\ell_k$ are not complementary, while $L_iL_k \notin E_\phi$ whenever $\ell_i$ and $\ell_k$ are complementary.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

The construction is polynomial in $n+m$ and depends only on the formula $\phi$. Therefore it can be carried out without knowing whether $\phi$ has a not-all-equal satisfying assignment. Moreover, the number of vertices and edges introduced is polynomial in the size of $\phi$.

The following lemma is the central combinatorial tool for proving the correctness of the reduction.

\begin{lemma}[Tail Lemma] \label{lemma::tail}
  Let $G$ be a connected graph containing four distinct vertices $a,b,c,d$ such that
  \[
  N_G(a)=\{b,c\},\quad N_G(b)=\{a,c,d\},\quad cd\in E_G,
  \]
  and suppose $c$ has a neighbour in $G$ outside $\{a,b,c,d\}$. If $H^2=G$, then
  \[
  N_H(d)\setminus\{a,b,c,d\} = N_G(c)\setminus\{a,b,c,d\}.
  \]
\end{lemma}
\input{subfiles/TailLemma.tex}

\begin{proof}
  Since $N_G(a)=\{b,c\}$, every neighbour of $a$ in $H$ is contained in $\{b,c\}$.
  
  First show that $N_H(a)=\{b\}$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $N_H(a)=\{c\}$. Then $ac \in E_H$. Because $ab \in E_G=E_{H^2}$, there is a path of length at most two from $a$ to $b$ in $H$. As $a$ only has the neighbour $c$, this forces $bc \in E_H$. 

  Now $cd \in E_G$, so $c$ and $d$ are at distance at most two in $H$. As $bc \in E_H$, the only possible $P_2$ from $c$ to $d$ uses $b$, and hence $bd \in E_H$. 

  Let $v \notin \{a,b,c,d\}$ be the neighbour of $c$ in $G$. Since $cv \in E_G$, there is a path of length at most two from $c$ to $v$ in $H$. This path cannot go through $a$, otherwise $av \in E_G$, contradicting $N_G(a)=\{b,c\}$. Nor can it go through $b$, since that would give $bv \in E_G$, contradicting $N_G(b)=\{a,c,d\}$. Therefore the path must be $c-d-v$, giving $dv \in E_H$.

  Since $bd, dv \in E_H$, have a path $b-d-v$ of length two in $H$, which implies $bv \in E_G$. This again contradicts $N_G(b)=\{a,c,d\}$. The contradiction eliminates the possibility $N_H(a)=\{c\}$, and consequently
  \begin{equation} \label{eq::tail_1}
    N_H(a)=\{b\}. 
  \end{equation}

  Now we determine the neighbourhood of $c$. Since $ab \in E_H$ as $N_H(a)=\{b\}$, and $ac\in E_G$, the edge $ac$ in $G=H^2$ must be realized by the path $a-b-c$. This gives that $bc \in E_H$. If $b$ had a neighbour $x \notin \{a,c\}$ in $H$, then $a-b-x$ would be a $P_2$, forcing $ax \in E_G$, which contradicts $N_H(a)=\{b\}$. Thus \begin{equation} \label{eq::tail_2}
    N_H(b)=\{a,c\}. 
  \end{equation}

  Since $bd\in E_G$ and $N_H(b)=\{a,c\}$, the $P_2$ from $b$ to $d$ in $H$ must be $b-c-d$ - it cannot use $a$, as $N_H(a)=\{b\}$. Therefore $cd \in E_H$. 
  
  If $c$ had a neighbour $y\notin\{b,d\}$ in $H$, then $b-c-y$ would give $by \in E_G$, contradicting Equation~\eqref{eq::tail_2}. Hence
  \begin{equation} \label{eq::tail_3}
    N_H(c)=\{b,d\}. 
  \end{equation}
  
  It remains to relate the external neighbours of $c$ in $G$ to those of $d$ in $H$. Take any $v\notin\{a,b,c,d\}$. If $cv\in E_G$, then there is a path of length at most two from $c$ to $v$ in $H$. By Equation~\eqref{eq::tail_3}, such a path must use $b$ or $d$ as the intermediate vertex. It cannot use $b$, since that would imply $bv \in E_G$, contradicting $N_G(b)=\{a,c,d\}$. Hence the path is $c-d-v$, so $dv \in E_H$. Conversely, if $dv \in E_H$, then $c-d-v$ is a $P_2$ in $H$ since $cd \in E_H$, giving $cv \in E_G$. 
  
  Therefore have established that for every $v\notin\{a,b,c,d\}$,
  \[
  v \in N_G(c) \text{ if and only if } v \in N_H(d).
  \]
  The desired set equality follows immediately.
\end{proof}

We now establish the two directions required for the validity of the reduction.

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::np_complete_forward}
  If $\phi$ is a \textbf{Yes}-instance of $\mathrm{NAESAT}$, then $G_\phi$ has a graph root.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch]
  Suppose that $\phi$ has an assignment satisfying not-all-equal. We construct a graph root $H=(V_\phi,E_H)$ on the same vertex set as $G_\phi$ as follows.

  The edges of $H$ consist of the edges of the tails, together with edges connecting $x$ to $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$. For each clause vertex $C_j$, connect $C_j$ to the three literal vertices corresponding to the literals contained in the clause, together with the edges required by its tail. Each literal vertex is connected to $\textsc{True}$ if its corresponding literal is assigned $\textsc{True}$ and to $\textsc{False}$ if its corresponding literal is assigned $\textsc{False}$. Finally, each literal vertex is connected to the appropriate literal-pair vertices, and each literal-pair vertex is connected to both $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$.

  Since the assignment is not-all-equal, every clause contains at least one $\textsc{True}$ literal and at least one $\textsc{False}$ literal. Thus, the construction produces the required adjacencies between the clause vertices and their literals. Moreover, complementary literals do not have a common neighbour in $H$, while every pair of non-complementary literals has a common neighbour through its corresponding literal-pair vertex. Hence, the literal adjacencies in $H^2$ are exactly those specified in $G_\phi$. The tail construction similarly ensures that all required tail adjacencies are obtained in $H^2$, while the connections to $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$ produce the remaining required adjacencies.

  Hence, $G_\phi=H^2$, and therefore $G_\phi$ has a graph root.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::np_complete_backward}
  If $G_\phi$ has a graph root, then $\phi$ is a \textbf{Yes}-instance of $\mathrm{NAESAT}$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch] 
  Suppose that $G_\phi$ has a graph root $H$, so that $G_\phi=H^2$. 
  
  Apply Lemma~\ref{lemma::tail} to the tail of $x$, with $(a,b,c,d)=(t_3,t_2,t_1,x)$. Since $t_1$ has the external neighbours $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$ in $G_\phi$, the lemma gives 
  \[ 
  N_H(x)\setminus\{t_3,t_2,t_1,x\} = \{\textsc{$\textsc{True}$},\textsc{$\textsc{False}$}\}. 
  \] 
  Thus, apart from its own tail, $x$ is adjacent in $H$ precisely to $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$. 
  
  Similarly, for each clause $C_j$, apply Lemma~\ref{lemma::tail} to its tail with $ (a,b,c,d)=(C_j^3,C_j^2,C_j^1,C_j)$. Since $C_j^1$ has the external neighbours $L_{j_1},L_{j_2},L_{j_3}$ in $G_\phi$, the lemma gives 
  \[ 
  N_H(C_j)\setminus \{C_j^3,C_j^2,C_j^1,C_j\} = \{L_{j_1},L_{j_2},L_{j_3}\}. 
  \] 
  Hence, apart from its own tail, $C_j$ is adjacent in $H$ precisely to the three literal vertices occurring in $C_j$. Since every literal vertex $L_i$ is adjacent to $x$ in $G_\phi=H^2$, each $L_i$ must be adjacent in $H$ to at least one of $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$. 
  
  On the other hand, complementary literal vertices are non-adjacent in $G_\phi$. Therefore they cannot have a common neighbour in $H$, and hence a literal and its complement cannot both be adjacent to the same one of $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$. It follows that every literal vertex is adjacent to exactly one of $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$, and complementary literals are adjacent to opposite constant vertices. 
  
  We may therefore define an assignment by declaring a literal $\ell_i$ to be $\textsc{True}$ only when $L_i\textsc{$\textsc{True}$}\in E_H$. Consider any clause $ C_j=\{\ell_{j_1},\ell_{j_2},\ell_{j_3}\}$. By construction, $C_j$ is adjacent in $G_\phi$ to both $\textsc{True}$ and $\textsc{False}$. Since, apart from its own tail, $C_j$ is adjacent in $H$ only to $L_{j_1},L_{j_2},L_{j_3}$, these three literal vertices must include one adjacent to $\textsc{True}$ and one adjacent to $\textsc{False}$. 
  
  Thus every clause contains at least one $\textsc{True}$ literal and at least one $\textsc{False}$ literal. Therefore the assignment is a not-all-equal satisfying assignment of $\phi$, and hence 
  \[ 
  \phi\in\mathrm{NAESAT}. 
  \] 
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[$\mathbf{NP}$-complete] \label{theorem::np_complete}
  Problem~\eqref{prob::square_problem} is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Theorem~\eqref{theorem::np} shows that Problem~\eqref{prob::square_problem} belongs to $\mathbf{NP}$. For $\mathbf{NP}$-hardness, consider the polynomial reduction from $\mathrm{NAESAT}$ to \textbf{SQUARE} given in Proof~\eqref{proof::polynomial_reduction}. By Lemma~\eqref{lemma::np_complete_forward}, every \textbf{Yes}-instance $\phi$ of $\mathrm{NAESAT}$ produces a graph $G_\phi$ with a graph root. Together with Lemma~\eqref{lemma::np_complete_backward}, this gives that $\mathrm{NAESAT} \leq_p \mathrm{SQUARE}$, so Problem~\eqref{prob::square_problem} is $\mathbf{NP}$-hard.

  Since Problem~\eqref{prob::square_problem} belongs to $\mathbf{NP}$ and is $\mathbf{NP}$-hard, it is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete.
\end{proof}

\section{Algorithm for Tree Roots} \label{sec::time_algorithm}

\citeauthor{ross_1960_square} give a necessary and sufficient characterization of graphs that are squares of trees, together with an algorithm for constructing the corresponding tree square root \cite{ross_1960_square}. The characterization is based on the maximal cliques of the square and the way in which these cliques intersect. We first establish the structural properties of the square of a tree that underlie the characterization, before describing their root finding algorithm.

\begin{theorem}[Structural Characterization of Squares of Trees] \label{theorem::structural_characterization_trees}
  Let $G$ be a finite, simple, undirected graph. Then $G = T^2$ for some tree $T$ if and only if either $G$ is a complete graph, or the maximal cliques $C_1, \ldots, C_n$ of $G$ satisfy the following conditions:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item\label{characterization::1} $G$ is connected and the closed neighbourhood of every vertex of $T$ forms a clique in $G$.
    \item\label{characterization::2} If two maximal cliques have exactly one common vertex $v$, then there is another maximal clique containing $v$ and one additional vertex from each of the two cliques.
    \item\label{characterization::3} Each maximal clique $C_i$ can be associated with a unique multicliqual vertex $b_i$. The number of multicliqual vertices contained in $C_i$ is equal to the number of maximal cliques containing $b_i$.
    \item\label{characterization::4} For any two maximal cliques $C_i$ and $C_j$, $|C_i \cap C_j| \leq 2$, that is their intersection contains at most two points 
    \item\label{characterization::5} The number of pairs of maximal cliques whose intersection contains two vertices is $n-1$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

To establish this characterization, let $G$ be the square of a tree $T$ and first consider several properties.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma::1}
  $G$ is complete if and only if $T$ is a star
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose that $T$ has a vertex $v$ that is adjacent to every other vertex. Then every pair of vertices in $T$ is at distance at most two, since any two vertices are either adjacent or have $v$ as a common neighbour. Hence $G = T^2$ is complete.

  Conversely, suppose that $G = T^2$ is complete. Then every pair of vertices in $T$ has distance at most two, so $\operatorname{diam}(T) \leq 2$. Since $T$ is a tree, this implies that there is a vertex $v$ adjacent to every other vertex. Thus, choosing $v$ as the root gives a rooted tree $T$ of depth one.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::2}
  $G$ is connected and the closed neighbourhood of every vertex of $T$ forms a clique in $G$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $v \in V_T$. Every pair of vertices in the closed neighbourhood $N_T[v]$ are either adjacent in $T$ or have $v$ as a common neighbour and thus has distance at most two in $T$. Hence $N_T[v]$ forms a clique in $G = T^2$. Furthermore, every edge of $T$ is also an edge of $G$, so $T$ is a spanning subgraph of $G$. Since $T$ is connected, this implies $G$ is also connected.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma::3}
  If $G$ is not complete, then the maximal cliques of $G$ are precisely the closed neighbourhoods of the non-leaf vertices of $T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $v$ be a non-leaf vertex of $T$. Since $v$ has at least two neighbours, every pair of vertices in $N_T(v)$ is at distance two through $v$. Hence $C(v) = N_T[v]$ is a clique in $G$. Now consider a vertex $u \notin N_T[v]$. The unique path from $u$ to $v$ in $T$ has length at least two. If this path has length greater than two, then $u$ is not adjacent in $G$ to $v$. If the path has length two, then $u$ can only be adjacent in $G$ to the vertices along the corresponding part of the path. In either case, $u$ cannot be added to $N_T[v]$ whilst maintaining a clique. Therefore $N_T[v]$ is a maximal clique.

  Conversely, let $C$ be a maximal clique of $G$. Since $G = T^2$, every pair of vertices in $C$ are at distance at most two in $T$. The vertices of such a clique must therefore be contained in the closed neighbourhood of a common non-leaf vertex $v$ of $T$. Thus $C \subseteq N_T[v]$. Since $N_T[v]$ is itself a clique and $C$ is maximal, this gives
  $C = N_T[v]$. Hence every maximal clique of $G$ is the closed neighbourhood of a non-leaf vertex of $T$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma::4}
  If $G$ is not complete, then a vertex $v \in V_G$ belongs to exactly one maximal clique if and only if $v$ is a leaf of $T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $v$ be a leaf of $T$, and let $u$ be its unique neighbour. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::3}, every maximal clique corresponds to the closed neighbourhood of a non-leaf vertex. Since $v$ has only one neighbour, the only maximal clique containing $v$ is $N_T[u]$.Thus $v$ belongs to exactly one maximal clique.

  Conversely, suppose that $v$ is not a leaf. Then $v$ has at least two neighbours in $T$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::3}, $N_T[v]$ is a maximal clique containing $v$. Since $G$ is not complete, Lemma~\ref{lemma::1} implies that $T$ is not a star. Hence at least one neighbour $u$ of $v$ is a non-leaf vertex. Therefore the closed neighbourhood $N_T[u]$ is another maximal clique containing $v$. Thus $v$ belongs to at least two maximal cliques and the vertices belonging to exactly one maximal clique are exactly the leaves of $T$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma::5}
  Let $C_i$ and $C_j$ be maximal cliques corresponding to non-leaf vertices $v_i$ and $v_j$ of $T$. Then $|C_i \cap C_j|=2$ if and only if $v_iv_j \in E_T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose that $v_i$ and $v_j$ are adjacent in $T$. Then both $v_i$ and $v_j$ belong to $
  N_T[v_i] \cap N_T[v_j]$. If there were another vertex $u$ in this intersection, then $u$ would be adjacent to both $v_i$ and $v_j$ in $T$. Together with the edge $v_i v_j$, this would create a cycle in $T$, which is impossible. Therefore, $|N_T[v_i] \cap N_T[v_j]|=2$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::3}, these closed neighbourhoods are the corresponding maximal cliques, so $|C_i \cap C_j| = 2$.

  Conversely, suppose that $|C_i \cap C_j| = 2$. Since $v_i$ and $v_j$ belong to their respective closed neighbourhoods, the intersection implies that $v_i$ and $v_j$ are either adjacent or have a common neighbour in $T$. If they are not adjacent, there would be a common neighbour $u$ adjacent to both $v_i$ and $v_j$. Then $v_i,v_j,u \in C_i \cap C_j$, which would give an intersection of at least three vertices, contradicting $|C_i \cap C_j| = 2$. Therefore, $v_i$ and $v_j$ must be adjacent with $v_iv_j \in E_T$. 
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma::6}
  $G$ is a $2$-edge-connected graph where the removal of any edge does not result in a disconnected graph
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $uv \in E_G$. Consider the edge $uv$ and the graph $G'$ with edge set $E_G \backslash \{uv\}$. Since $G = T^2$, either $uv \in E_T$, or there exists a vertex $w \in V_T$ such that $uw,vw \in E_T$. In the latter case, $u$ and $v$ are connected by the path $u-w-v$ in $G'$.

  If $uv \in E_T$, then the vertices closest to $u$ and $v$ along the remaining components of $T-uv$ are adjacent in $G$. Thus, the two components of $T-uv$ remain connected through vertices that are joined by edges in $G-uv$.

  Consequently, removing $uv$ from $G$ does not disconnect the graph. Hence $G$ has no bridges and is $2$-edge-connected.
\end{proof}

Combining gives the following proof of Theorem~\eqref{theorem::structural_characterization_trees}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose first that $G = T^2$ for some tree $T$. If $G$ is complete, then Lemma~\ref{lemma::1} shows that $T$ is a star.

  Now suppose that $G$ is not complete. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::2}, $G$ is connected and every closed neighbourhood in $T$ forms a clique in $G$, giving \hyperref[characterization::1]{(i)}. 

  By Lemma~\ref{lemma::3}, each maximal clique of $G$ is the closed neighbourhood of a non-leaf vertex of $T$. Suppose that two maximal cliques $N_T[u]$ and $N_T[v]$ have exactly one common vertex $x$. Since $T$ is a tree, $u$ and $v$ cannot be adjacent, and hence $x$ lies on the unique path between $u$ and $v$. Thus, $x$ is adjacent in $T$ to both $u$ and $v$, so $N_T[x]$ forms a maximal clique containing $x$, together with the additional vertices $u$ and $v$ from the two original cliques. This gives \hyperref[characterization::2]{(ii)}.

  For a maximal clique $C_i=N_T[b_i]$, the associated vertex $b_i$ is the corresponding non-leaf vertex of $T$. Every non-leaf neighbour of $b_i$ in $T$ gives another maximal clique containing $b_i$, while each such neighbour is itself also a multicliqual vertex contained in $C_i$. Consequently, the number of multicliqual vertices contained in $C_i$ is equal to the number of maximal cliques containing $b_i$, giving \hyperref[characterization::3]{(iii)}.
  
  By Lemma~\ref{lemma::5}, two maximal cliques intersect in two vertices exactly when their corresponding non-leaf vertices are adjacent in $T$. Since $T$ contains no cycles, two such closed neighbourhoods cannot have more than two vertices in common. This gives \hyperref[characterization::4]{(iv)}. Finally, the non-leaf vertices of $T$, together with the edges between them, form a tree. If there are $n$ such vertices, there are exactly $n-1$ edges between them. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::5}, each such edge corresponds to a pair of maximal cliques with an intersection containing exactly two vertices. Hence \hyperref[characterization::5]{(v)} holds.

  Conversely, suppose that $G$ is not complete and that its maximal cliques $C_1,\ldots,C_n$ satisfy \hyperref[characterization::1]{(i)}--\hyperref[characterization::5]{(v)}. By \hyperref[characterization::3]{(iii)}, we may associate each maximal clique $C_i$ with a unique vertex $b_i$ that belongs to multiple maximal cliques. These vertices will form the internal vertices of the tree root. Construct $T$ with vertex set $V_T=\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}$. For every pair of maximal cliques satisfying $|C_i\cap C_j|=2$, 
  add the edge $b_i b_j$ to $T$. By \hyperref[characterization::5]{(v)}, $T$ contains exactly $n-1$ edges, and the intersection structure given by the characterization ensures that these edges connect all vertices without forming a cycle. Thus $T$ is a tree. It remains to add the unicliqual vertices. By \hyperref[characterization::3]{(iii)}, these vertices correspond to leaves of the root. For each clique $C_i$, attach all vertices of $C_i$ that occur in no other maximal clique as leaves adjacent to $b_i$. The resulting graph is a tree $T$. Each maximal clique $C_i$ is represented by the closed neighbourhood of $b_i$ in $T$, while the remaining vertices are attached as leaves to their corresponding internal vertices. Therefore every edge of $G$ is represented by two vertices at distance at most two in $T$, and no additional edges are introduced. Hence $T^2=G$ and $G$ is the square of a tree.
\end{proof}

The proof of Theorem~\eqref{theorem::structural_characterization_trees} gives the following algorithm: Given a graph known to be the square of a tree, identify all maximal cliques, classify the vertices as multicliqual or unicliqual according to the number of maximal cliques in which they occur, and construct the tree $T$ by connecting the multicliqual vertices to form the internal structure of $T$ and attaching the unicliqual vertices as leaves to their corresponding multicliqual vertices.

\begin{algorithm} \caption{Ross-Harary Tree Construction}  \label{algo::ross_harary_tree} 
  \begin{algorithmic}[1] 
    \State \textbf{Input:} A graph $G$ where $G = T^2$
    \State \textbf{Output:} A tree $T$
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \Statex \textbf{Step 1}
    \If{$G = K_p$ is complete}
        \State \textbf{Return} tree $T$ with depth $1$ where root can be any vertex
    \Else
        \State \textbf{Continue} 
    \EndIf
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \Statex \textbf{Step 2}
    \State Identify all maximal cliques $C_1, \dots, C_n$ of $G$ using an algorithm such as Bron-Kerbosch \cite{bron_kerbosch_1973_maximal_cliques} where $n > 1$ from \hyperref[characterization::1]{Theorem~\ref{theorem::structural_characterization_trees} (i)}
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \Statex \textbf{Step 3}
    \State Let $b_1, \dots, b_n$ be multicliqual points corresponding to $C_1, \dots, C_n$ formed from 
    \hyperref[characterization::3]{Theorem~\ref{theorem::structural_characterization_trees}  (iii)} 
    \State Construct a graph $T$ with vertex set $\{b_1, \dots, b_n\}$
    \For{each pair $(C_i, C_j)$ satisfying $|C_i \cap C_j| = 2$  }
        \State Add edge between $b_i$ and $b_j$ in $T$
    \EndFor
    \State By \hyperref[characterization::5]{Theorem~\ref{theorem::structural_characterization_trees}  (v)}, $T$ is a tree

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \Statex \textbf{Step 4}
    \For{each clique $C_i$}
        \State Let $n_i$ be the number of unicliqual points in $C_i$
        \State Attach $n_i$ leaf nodes to $b_i$ in $T$
    \EndFor
    \State The resulting tree $T$ has the same number of nodes as $G$ and satisfies $T^2 = G$
  \end{algorithmic} 
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Tree Root Algorithm] \label{theorem::tree_root_algorithm}
  Given a graph $G = T^2$, Algorithm~\eqref{algo::ross_harary_tree} returns a tree $T$ such that $T^2=G$. Furthermore, this tree is unique up to isomorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  If $G$ is complete, Step 1 returns a star. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::1}, this is a tree square root of $G$. Otherwise, Lemma~\ref{lemma::3} shows that each maximal clique corresponds to a non-leaf vertex of the root. Thus the vertices $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ identified in Step 3 determine the internal vertices of $T$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma::5}, two of these internal vertices are adjacent precisely when their corresponding maximal cliques have an intersection of size two. Therefore Step 3 recovers all edges between the internal vertices. The remaining vertices belong to exactly one maximal clique and, by Lemma~\ref{lemma::4}, correspond to leaves. Step 4 therefore places each of these vertices at its unique position in the tree. Hence the algorithm reconstructs a tree $T$ satisfying $T^2=G$. Since the internal vertices, their adjacencies, and the attachment of every leaf are all determined by the maximal cliques of $G$, the resulting tree is unique up to isomorphism.
\end{proof}

While Algorithm~\ref{algo::ross_harary_tree} formalizes the original $1960$ constructive proof by \citeauthor{ross_1960_square}, it predates the formalization of computational complexity classes $\mathcal{P}$ vs. $\mathcal{NP}$ established in the early $1970$s by \citeauthor{cook_1971_complexity}~\parencite{cook_1971_complexity}
and \citeauthor{karp_1972_reducibility}~\parencite{karp_1972_reducibility}. From a modern perspective, Ross and Harary's strategy relies on global maximal clique enumeration to group vertices into local star subgraphs. If implemented using general-purpose clique algorithms such as Bron-Kerbosch, this approach suffers from exponential worst-case execution time.

In contrast, \citeauthor{lin_1995_square_roots} bypass clique enumeration entirely by shifting to a local, bottom-up leaf-trimming strategy to give a polynomial algorithm \cite{lin_1995_square_roots}. Rather than searching for global subgraphs, they identify leaves of $T$ directly as simplicial vertices with minimal degree, partition their closed neighborhoods into leaf and non-leaf sets, and use the structural properties of these local neighborhoods to reconstruct parent-child adjacencies directly. By repeatedly trimming identified leaves, they reduce the problem to smaller tree squares until the tree structure is fully recovered.

\section{Mathematical Optimization Approach} \label{sec::mathematical_optimization_approach}

The two methods so far have primarily focused on the definition of a square of a graph to be paths of length at most than two defined in Definition~\eqref{def::square_of_a_graph}. Using adjacency matrices, this definition is equivalent expressed as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq::fund_adj_square_or}
  A_G = (A_H)^2 \lor A_H.
\end{equation}
where $\lor$ is applied entrywise to the matrices. This concise interpretation with the logical operator lends itself easily to a SAT problem whereby one could introduce Boolean variables for the edges of $H$ and encode $\displaystyle (A_H)^2_{ij} = \big( (A_H)_{i1} \land (A_H)_{1j} \big) \lor \big( (A_H)_{i2} \land (A_H)_{2j} \big) \lor \cdots \lor \big( (A_H)_{in} \land (A_H)_{nj} \big)$. Thus, for each pair of distinct vertices $i,j$, the condition that $ij$ is an edge of $G$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation} \label{eq::sat_square}
  g_{ij} \text{ if and only if } \big( \bigwedge_k (x_{ik} \land x_{kj}) \lor x_{ij} \big). 
\end{equation}
The resulting Boolean formula can be converted into a standard SAT instance using auxiliary variables and a polynomial number of clauses. Hence, the logical formulation provides a polynomial-size SAT representation of the graph-square condition.

However, constructing a polynomial-size SAT instance does not imply that the underlying problem can be solved in polynomial time. A general SAT instance may require exponential time to solve, since SAT is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete. Therefore, to obtain a polynomial-time algorithm through this approach, one would additionally need to show that the SAT instances arising from Equation~\eqref{eq::sat_square} belongs to a tractable subclass of SAT, such as $2$-SAT, or otherwise provide a polynomial-time algorithm for solving these particular instances. The formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq::sat_square} evidently does not belong to $2$-SAT, as each constraint involves a disjunction over all possible common neighbours $k$ and therefore contains terms involving more than two Boolean variables. More generally, one would suspect from the results in \ref{sec::np_completeness_proof} that there is no immediate polynomial-time algorithm for solving this instance and any restricted version of the graph root finding problem would necessitate significant machinery.  

Instead, we aim to situate the problem completely within a polynomial-time framework by considering another equivalent representation of Equation~\eqref{eq::fund_adj_square_or} and its equivalent formulation in Definition~\eqref{def::square_of_a_graph}.

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::G_equal_to_H^2}
  Let $G$ and $H$ be graphs with adjacency matrices $A_G$ and $A_H$,respectively
  The graph $H$ is a root of $G$ if and only if 
  Equation~\ref{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected} holds
  \[ 
  A_G = \mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\!\big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big).
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof} 
  For $i \neq j$, since $D_H$ is diagonal, have $(D_H)_{ij} = 0$ and hence
  \[
  \big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big)_{ij} = (A_H)^2_{ij} + (A_H)_{ij}.
  \]

  Suppose $ij \in E_G$ such that $(A_H)^2_{ij} + (A_H)_{ij} \geq 1$. This implies that at least on of $(A_H)_{ij} = 1$ or $(A_H)^2_{ij} \geq 1$ must be $\textsc{True}$. If $(A_H)_{ij} = 1$, then $d_H(i,j) = 1$.  If $(A_H)^2_{ij} \geq 1$, then there exists a vertex $k$ such that $ik, kj \in E_H$, and hence $d_H(i,j) \leq 2$. Therefore $1 \leq d_H(i,j) \leq 2$, and thus $ij \in E_{H^2}$.

  Suppose $ij \in E_{H^2}$ such that $1 \leq d_H(i,j) \leq 2$. $D_H$ is diagonal such that $(D_H)_{ij} = 0$. If $d_H(i,j) = 1$, then $(A_H)_{ij} = 1$. If $d_H(i,j) = 2$, then there exists a vertex $k$ such that $ik, kj \in E_H$, and hence $(A_H)^2_{ij} \geq 1$. In either case, $(A_H)^2_{ij} + (A_H)_{ij} \geq 1$, so $(A_G)_{ij} = 1$, and hence $ij \in E_G$.

  For $i = j$, since all graphs are simple, have $(E_{\cdot})_{ii} = 0$, $(A_{\cdot})_{ii} = 0$ and hence
  \[
  \big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big)_{ii} = (A_H)^2_{ii} - (D_H)_{ii}.
  \]
  $(A_H)^2_{ii} - (D_H)_{ii} = 0$ is always $\textsc{True}$ since the degree matrix of $H$ always has diagonal values $\displaystyle (D_H)_{ii} = \sum_k x_{ik}$ and each edge from vertex $i$ to its neighbour in $H$ will always create a loop to itself in the square and so $\displaystyle (A_H)^2_{ii} = 
  \sum_{k = 1} x_{ik}x_{ki} = \sum_{k = 1} x_{ik}^2$ by the symmetry of adjacency matrix $A_H$. 
  
  Since $x_{ik} \in \{0, 1\}$, the square of the boolean is identical to the boolean $x_ik$ such that $\displaystyle (A_H)^2_{ii} = \sum_k x_{ik} = (D_H)_{ii}$ and $(A_G)_{ii} = \mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big)$ as required. 

  Combining both gives that $E_G = E_{H^2}$, and so $G = H^2$.  
\end{proof}

The formulation of the adjacency matrix of the graph square in Equation~\eqref{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected} is very powerful. However, the presence of the indicator function introduces a nonlinearity that prevents the problem from being expressed as a purely quadratic form, thereby limiting the direct applicability of existing results from the quadratic optimization literature.

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::no_indicator_girth_5}
  If $H$ has girth $5$, then Equation~\eqref{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected} can be simplified to become 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected_no_indicator}
    A_G = \big((A_H)^2 - D_H + A_H\big).
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Since the graph root has girth $5$ there are no cycles of length $3$ or $4$ in $H$. Thus for every pair of vertices $i \neq j \in V$ there is either no cycle that contains both $i$ and $j$ or $i$ and $j$ lay on a common cycle of length at least $5$. In all cases $(D_H)_{ij} = 0$. If there is no cycle, then there is at most one path between $i$ and $j$. 
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
    \item If the path is of length $1$, then $(A_H)_{ij} = 1, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 0$ and so $(A_G)_{ij} = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1$.
    \item If the path is of length $2$, then $(A_H)_{ij} = 0, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 1$ and so $(A_G)_{ij} = 1 - 0 - 0 = 1$.
    \item If the path is of length $\geq 3$ or a path does not exist, then $(A_H)_{ij} = 0, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 0 \implies (A_G)_{ij} = 0$.
  \end{enumerate}
  If $i$ and $j$ lay on a common cycle of length at least five then there are two paths between $i,j$
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
    \item If one path is of length $1$, then the other is of length $\geq 4$ and hence $(A_H)_{ij} = 1, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 0 \implies (A_G)_{ij} = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1$.
    \item If one path is of length $2$, then the other is of length $\geq 3$ and hence $(A_H)_{ij} = 0, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 1 \implies (A_G)_{ij} = 1 - 0 - 0 = 1$.
    \item If the paths are both of length $\geq 3$,  then $(A_H)_{ij} = 0, (A_H)^2_{ij} = 0 \implies (A_G)_{ij} = 0$. 
  \end{enumerate}
  If $i = j$, then $(A_H)_{ii} = 0$, $(D_H)_{ii} = \deg_H(i)$, and $(A_H)^2_{ii} = \deg_H(i)$. Thus,
  \[
  (A_H)^2_{ii} - (D_H)_{ii} + (A_H)_{ii} = \deg_H(i) - \deg_H(i) + 0 = 0 = (A_G)_{ii}.
  \]
\end{proof}

We will not focus on this particular restricted class and analyze the integer program generated by Equation~\eqref{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected_no_indicator}, but rather aim for a unified method for solving the problem in general. Nevertheless this result gives strong intuition into the results established by \citeauthor{farzad_2009_roots} in \cite{farzad_2009_roots} in constructing a polynomial time algorithm for recognizing graph roots where $H$ has girth at least $6$. 

\subsection{Formulation} \label{subsec::integer_program_formulation}

Using Lemma~\eqref{lemma::G_equal_to_H^2}, we construct an integer program to solve instances of \textbf{SQUARE}. The set of decision variables is
\[
X=\{x_{ij}\mid i,j\in V_G\},
\]
where $x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the corresponding entry of the adjacency matrix $A_H$. In a feasible solution, $x_{ij}=1$ if and only if $ij\in E_H$. The variables $y_{ikj}\in\{0,1\}$ represent the products $x_{ik}x_{kj}$ in $(A_H)^2_{ij}$, while $z_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}$ represents the argument of the indicator function.

Given an input $G$ to \textbf{SQUARE}, our goal is to construct the following matrices, where $g_{ij}$ is the corresponding entry of the adjacency matrix of $G$.

$\displaystyle 
D_H =
\begin{bmatrix}
\deg_H(1) & 0      & \cdots & 0 & 0      \\
0         & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots    & \cdots & \deg_H(i) & \cdots & 0      \\
0         & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0         & \cdots & 0      & \cdots & \deg_H(n)
\end{bmatrix}, 
$ 
$\displaystyle A_H =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1j} & \cdots & x_{1n} \\
\vdots  & \ddots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots  \\
x_{i1} & \cdots & x_{ij} & \cdots & x_{in} \\
\vdots  & \ddots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots  \\
x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{nj} & \cdots & x_{nn}
\end{bmatrix}
$ such that 
\[
(A_H)^2 = \begin{bmatrix}
\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{k1} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{kj} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{kn} \\
\vdots  & \ddots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots  \\
\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{ik}x_{k1} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{ik}x_{kj} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{ik}x_{kn} \\
\vdots  & \ddots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots  \\
\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{nk}x_{k1} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{nk}x_{kj} & \cdots & \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n x_{nk}x_{kn}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Then with known constants $g_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ from $A_G$, we can formulate \eqref{eq::fund_adj_eq_undirected} as an integer program by equating entries of each matrix to give the following linear system of $n^2$ equations.
\begin{equation}  \label{eq::fund_programming_equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
g_{11} \\
\vdots \\
g_{1j} \\
\vdots \\
g_{1n} \\
\vdots \\
g_{ij} \\
\vdots \\
g_{nn}
\end{bmatrix} 
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(z_{11}\big) \\
\vdots \\
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(z_{1j}\big) \\
\vdots \\
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(z_{1n}\big) \\
\vdots \\
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(z_{ij}\big) \\
\vdots \\
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(z_{nn}\big)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{k1} - (D_H)_{11} + x_{11} \big) \\
\vdots \\ \displaystyle
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{kj} - (D_H)_{1j} + x_{1j} \big) \\
\vdots \\ \displaystyle
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^n x_{1k}x_{kn} - (D_H)_{1n} + x_{1n} \big)\\
\vdots \\ \displaystyle
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^n x_{ik}x_{kj} - (D_H)_{ij} + x_{ij} \big)\\
\vdots \\ \displaystyle
\mathbbm{1}_{\geq 1}\big(\sum_{k=1}^n x_{nk}x_{kn} - (D_H)_{nn} + x_{nn} \big)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The matrix $D_H$ is diagonal can be represented as $\displaystyle (D_H)_{ii} = \sum_{k} x_{ik}$ where $(D_H)_{ij} = 0$ for $i \neq j$. The variable $z_{ij}$ represent the terms inside the indicator function such that
\[
g_{ij} = 1 \text{ if and only if } z_{ij} \geq 1 \text{ and } g_{ij} = 0 \text{ if and only if } z_{ij} = 0.
\]

\chapter{Square of a Tree using Linear Programming} \label{chap::square_of_a_tree_LP}

In this chapter, we explore the recognition of squares of trees using linear programming. We first introduce a linear programming relaxation for $\mathcal{IP}(G)$ and investigate the structure of its feasible solutions. We then consider additional constraints that can be derived from the structure of the input graph and used to augment the relaxation. The resulting formulations are tested computationally on a collection of graphs in order to evaluate their ability to recover integral solutions corresponding to tree roots.

The methodology used in this work is implemented using the SageMath~\cite{sagemath} framework with Gurobi~\cite{gurobi} as the optimisation backend. The barrier method is used, as seen in Code~\ref{lst::linear_program}--\ref{lst::unicliqual_rounding_linear_program}, through the parameter \texttt{Method=2}. The barrier method is an interior-point method that uses a predictor-corrector scheme based on Newton iterations to approach an optimal solution from within the feasible region. For linear programs, classical interior-point theory provides polynomial worst-case bounds on the number of iterations, with each iteration requiring polynomial computational effort in the number of variables and constraints \cite{gonzaga_1991_barrier_function}. Thus, the barrier method provides a theoretically polynomial approach for solving the LP formulations considered in this chapter, although these worst-case bounds do not necessarily reflect the practical running time of the implementation. 

Throughout this chapter, we consider graphs as unlabelled graphs, so graphs that are isomorphic are regarded as the same graph instance. However, the algorithms themselves operate on a labelled representation of each graph. Each unlabelled graph is therefore represented computationally by a particular labelled graph generated by SageMath, with the assigned vertex labels retained throughout the subsequent computations.

The particular labelling depends on the graph generation procedure. Consequently, different labellings of the same unlabelled graph, or different generated representatives of the same isomorphism class, may lead to different intermediate or selected solutions of the optimisation procedures, even though the underlying graph structure is unchanged. The results reported in this chapter therefore correspond to the particular labelled representatives produced by the stated generation procedure. The complete test harness and instance generation scripts are available at \href{https://github.com/Hellwoodoo/Graph-Squares}{\texttt{github.com/Hellwoodoo/Graph-Squares}}.

\section{Linear Program Relaxation} \label{sec::linear_program_relaxation}

We construct the linear relaxation of the integer program defined by Equations~\eqref{eq::IP_x_variable}--\eqref{eq::IP_diagonal_x}, which generates a feasible weighted solution in polynomial time. The relaxation has identical constraints, however the decision variables \eqref{eq::IP_x_variable}, \eqref{eq::IP_y_variable} and \eqref{eq::IP_z_variable} are replaced with
\begin{alignat}{3}
& x_{ij} \in  \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, 
&&\qquad \forall i,j \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \label{eq::LP_x_variable} \\
& y_{ikj} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, 
&&\qquad \forall i,k,j \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \label{eq::LP_y_variable} \\
& z_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, 
&&\qquad \forall i,j \in \{1, \dots, n\} \label{eq::LP_z_variable}. 
\end{alignat} 

This linear program on the arbitrary graph $G$ using Equations~\eqref{eq::LP_x_variable}--\eqref{eq::LP_z_variable} and Equations~\eqref{eq::IP_fund_matrix}--\eqref{eq::IP_diagonal_x} is denoted as $\mathcal{LP}(G)$. 

Such a linear program may have \emph{integral solutions} and \emph{fractional solutions} when the root exists and \emph{fractional solutions} and \emph{infeasible solutions} when the root does not exist. 

\begin{definition}[Integral Solutions]
  A feasible solution to the linear program is an \emph{integral solution} if
  \[
  x_{ij} \in \{0,1\} \quad \forall i,j \in V.
  \]
  In this case, the variables $x_{ij}$ define a graph root $H$ through
  \[
  E_H = \{(i,j) \in V \times V : x_{ij}=1\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

An integral solution to $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is a feasible solution for $\mathcal{IP}(G)$. 

\begin{definition}[Fractional Solutions]
  A feasible solution to the linear program is a \emph{fractional solution}
  if
  \[
  x_{ij} \in [0,1] \quad \forall i,j \in V,
  \]
  and there exists at least one pair $(i,j)$ such that $0 < x_{ij} < 1$. In this case, the variables $x_{ij}$ do not directly define a graph root.
\end{definition}

In Figure~\ref{fig::ip_lp_set_geometric} solutions which are $LP$ feasible may be integral or fractional. The results in Table~\ref{tab::linear_program_outcomes} demonstrate both cases. When $H$ exists, an integral $LP$ solution corresponds to a feasible point in $\mathcal{F}*{IP}$, while a fractional $LP$ solution lies in $\mathcal{F}*{LP} \setminus \mathcal{F}_{IP}$. For $n=3$ and $n=4$, all instances for which $H$ exists have integral $LP$ solutions. From $n=5$ onwards, fractional solutions begin to occur, with their number increasing as $n$ increases.

When $H$ does not exist, two outcomes are observed. An $LP$-infeasible instance has no point in $\mathcal{F}*{LP}$, and therefore cannot contain a feasible integer solution. Alternatively, the $LP$ may remain feasible but return a fractional solution, placing the solution in $\mathcal{F}*{LP} \setminus \mathcal{F}*{IP}$. The number of such fractional solutions also increases with $n$. 

These results demonstrate that $LP$ feasibility alone is insufficient to establish the existence of a graph root, since $\mathcal{F}_{LP}$ may contain both integral and fractional solutions. This is to be expected as LPs are solvable in polynomial time, but \textbf{SQUARE} is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete. Fractional solutions arise because the linear relaxation weakens the integrality restrictions of the integer program, allowing variables that are binary in the integer formulation to take values in the continuous interval $[0,1]$.

The following examines which properties of the integer formulation are preserved under the linear relaxation and identifies where fractional solutions can lose their interpretation as graph roots. Recall Equations~\eqref{eq::IP_product_upper_first}--\eqref{eq::IP_product_lower}, which impose $y_{ikj} = x_{ik}x_{kj}$. When $x$ is binary, the construction $y$ is binary as well; however, this may cause issues when $x$ is fractional.

Recall Equation~\eqref{eq::IP_fund_matrix}, where $\displaystyle z_{ii} = \sum_{k = 1}^{n} y_{iki} - \sum_{k = 1}^{n} x_{ik} + x_{ii}$ can be simplified to $\displaystyle z_{ii} = \sum_{k = 1}^{n} x_{ik}^2 - \sum_{k = 1}^{n} x_{ik}$ and is equal to $0$ when $x$ is binary. When $x_{ik}$ is fractional, $x_{ik}^2 \leq x_{ik}$, such that $\displaystyle z_{ii} = \sum_{k = 1}^{n} (x_{ik}^2 - x_{ik}) \leq 0$. As $z \in \mathbb{Z}{\geq 0}$, this gives that $z{ii} = 0$ as required, and no additional modifications are required.

However, recall Equation~\eqref{eq::IP_indicator_arg}, where $\displaystyle z_{ij} = \sum_{k = 1}^{n} x_{ik}x_{kj} + x_{ij}$. When $z_{ij} = 0$, the equation follows the standard definition of graph squares, such that $x_{ij} = 0$ and, for all intermediary vertices $k$, at least one of $x_{ik}$ or $x_{kj}$ must be equal to $0$. However, when $z_{ij} \geq 1$, $x_{ij}$ and $x_{ik}x_{kj}$ may satisfy the constraints without giving a valid integral path in the root. Thus, the linear relaxation may remain feasible even when no graph root exists.

The following proposition establishes that whenever a graph root exists, the linear relaxation will never return an infeasible solution. 

\begin{proposition} \label{prop::linear_relaxation_feasibility}
  If $G$ has a square root $H$, then $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is feasible. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose that $G$ has a square root $H$. By Theorem~\ref{theorem::IP_gives_root}, the adjacency matrix $A_H$ gives a feasible solution to $\mathcal{IP}(G)$. Since $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is obtained by relaxing the integrality constraints of $\mathcal{IP}(G)$, every feasible solution of $\mathcal{IP}(G)$ is also feasible for $\mathcal{LP}(G)$. Hence $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is feasible.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition} \label{prop::unique_maximal_clique_minimizer}
  Let $G$ be a graph and let $C\cong K_k$ be a maximal clique of $G$.
  Consider the clique-restricted problem induced by the constraints
  \[
  \sum_{j\in V_C\setminus\{i\}}x_{ij}\ge1, \quad i\in V_C.
  \]
  Among solutions symmetric on $C$, the unique minimizer of
  \[
  \sum_{ij\in E_C}x_{ij}
  \]
  is
  \[
  x_{ij}=\frac{1}{k-1} \quad \forall ij\in E_C.
  \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Restrict to solutions symmetric on $C$, so that
  \[
  x_{ij}=t
  \qquad\forall ij\in E_C
  \]
  for some $t\ge0$. Since every vertex of $C\cong K_k$ has
  $k-1$ neighbours in $C$, each clique constraint becomes
  \[
  (k-1)t\ge1.
  \]
  Hence
  \[
  t\ge\frac{1}{k-1}.
  \]
  The clique-restricted objective is
  \[
  \sum_{ij\in E_C}x_{ij}
  =\binom{k}{2}t.
  \]
  Since this is increasing in $t$, it is minimized by the smallest
  feasible value,
  \[
  t=\frac{1}{k-1}.
  \]
  Therefore the unique symmetric minimizer is
  \[
  x_{ij}=\frac{1}{k-1}
  \qquad\forall ij\in E_C.
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}[Fractional Cliques in $\min \mathcal{LP}(G)$]
  Let $G$ be a graph and let $x$ be an optimal solution of
  $\min\mathcal{LP}(G)$. For each edge $ij\in E_G$, define
  \[
  t_{ij}
  =
  \min\{|C|: C\text{ is a maximal clique of }G,\ ij\in E_C\}.
  \]
  Then
  \[
  x_{ij}=\frac{1}{t_{ij}-1}
  \qquad\forall ij\in E_G.
  \]
\end{conjecture}

\section{Leaf Fixing} \label{sec::leaf_fixing}

Two important structures in a tree are used to construct the linear program that fixes the edges of the tree uniquely.

\begin{definition}[Sparse Pendant] \label{def::sparse_pendant}
  Let $T$ be a tree. A \textbf{sparse pendant} is a path of length three, $s,u,v$ where $s$ is a leaf and $u$ has degree $2$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Pendant Fork] \label{def::pendant_fork}
  Let $T$ be a tree. A \textbf{pendant fork} is the induced subgraph of $T$ on four vertices $s_1,s_2,u,v$ where $s_1,s_2$ are leaves adjacent to $u$, $v$ is a neighbour of $u$ that is not a leaf.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::tree_pendant_pendant_fork}
  Every tree with at least $3$ vertices contains either a \hyperref[def::sparse_pendant]{sparse pendant} or a \hyperref[def::pendant_fork]{pendant fork} subgraph.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof} 
  Let $T$ be a rooted tree with $|V_T| \ge 3$, and root $T$ at an arbitrary vertex. Since $T$ is finite, there exists a leaf, say $s$, of maximum depth. Let $u$ be the parent of $s$. Every child of $u$ is a leaf, since if there was some child $w$ of $u$ that is not a leaf, then $w$ would have a child and hence there would exist a vertex at depth strictly greater than that of $s$, contradicting the choice of $s$.

  If $u$ is not the root, let $v$ denote its parent. Every neighbour of $u$ other than $v$ is a child of $u$, and hence is a leaf. If $u$ has exactly one such leaf neighbour, say $s$, then $u$ has exactly two neighbours, namely $s$ and $v$, and hence $\deg_T(u)=2$. Therefore, the induced subgraph on
  \[
  \{s,u,v\}
  \]
  forms a pendant subgraph.

  Otherwise $u$ has at least two leaf neighbours $s_1,s_2$. Choose another neighbour $v$ of $u$ distinct from $s_1,s_2$. If $u$ is not the root, choose $v$ as its parent. Since $v$ is distinct from $s_1$ and $s_2$, have
  \[
  \{s_1,s_2,v\}\subseteq N(u),
  \]
  and $\deg_T(u)\ge3$. Hence the induced subgraph on
  \[
  \{s_1,s_2,u,v\}
  \]
  forms a pendant fork subgraph.

  If $u$ is the root, then all neighbours of $u$ are children and hence are leaves. Since $|V_T| \ge 3$, $u$ has at least two neighbours. If it has exactly two neighbours, the induced subgraph on these three vertices forms a sparse pendant. Otherwise, choose any three distinct children $s_1,s_2,v$ of $u$. Then
  \[
    \{s_1,s_2,v\}\subseteq N_T(u),
  \]
  and the induced subgraph on
  \[
    \{s_1,s_2,u,v\}
  \]
  forms a pendant fork subgraph.
\end{proof}

Using this lemma, every tree contains either a pendant or a pendant fork subgraph. Consequently, whenever $G$ admits a tree root, we may identify one of these two configurations in the square and use this identification to derive valid fixings for the variables $x_{ij}$.

\subsection{Single Triangular Decomposition} \label{subsec::single_triangular_decomposition}
We first consider how to identify the sparse pendant described in Definition~\ref{def::sparse_pendant} from $G$. For a vertex $s \in V$, define
\[
T(s) = \bigl\{ \{u,v\} \subseteq V \setminus \{s\} \vert (s,u)\in E,\ (s,v)\in E,\ (u,v)\in E \bigr\}
\]
to be the set of $K_3$s in $G$ that contain $s$. If $|T(s)| = 1$ where $T(s) = \{\{u,v\}\}$ then the vertex $s$ exists in a unique $K_3$ of $G$. When the neighbours have different degree $\deg_G(u) \neq \deg_G(v)$, then the vertex with smaller degree must be the stem vertex in the square root. This is assigned deterministically for a single triangle $s$ using
\begin{equation} \label{eq::single_triangle_fix}
x_{sa} = x_{as} = x_{ab} = x_{ba} = 1,
\quad
\text{where }
(a,b) =
\begin{cases}
(u,v), & \deg_G(u) \leq \deg_G(v), \\
(v,u), & \deg_G(u) > \deg_G(v).
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

If $\deg_G(u) = \deg_G(v)$, then either $u,v$ can be chosen since they are connected to a clique.

\begin{proposition}[Single Triangle Decomposition Interchangeability] \label{prop::single_triangular_decomposition_equality}
  Let $s,u,v$ form a $K_3$ in square $G$ where $s$ only lies in a single clique. If $\deg_G(u)=\deg_G(v)=d\geq2$, then one can choose to assign
  \[
  x_{su}=x_{us}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1
  \]
  in the tree root $H$ without loss of generality.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Since $s$ lies in a single maximal clique in $G$, in any tree root $H$, the vertex $s$ must be a leaf, with exactly one of $u$ or $v$ being the internal vertex adjacent to the remainder of the graph. Assume without loss of generality that $u$ is this internal vertex. Since $v$ has no other connections in $H$ besides $s$ and $u$, any vertex adjacent to $v$ in $G$ other than $s$ must also be adjacent to $u$ in $G$. Hence
  \[
  N_G(v)\setminus\{s\}\subseteq N_G(u)\setminus\{s\}.
  \]
  Since $\deg_G(u)=\deg_G(v)$, the two sets have equal cardinality, and therefore
  \[
  N_G(u)\setminus\{s\}=N_G(v)\setminus\{s\}.
  \]
  Thus $u$ and $v$ have identical neighbourhoods outside $s$ and may be interchanged without changing the square graph $G$. Consequently, we may choose $u$ as the internal vertex and assign
  \[
  x_{su}=x_{us}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1
  \]
  without loss of generality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Tetrahedron Decomposition} \label{subsec::tetrahedron_decomposition}
We now consider how to identify the pendant fork described in Definition~\ref{def::pendant_fork} from $G$. 

A natural extension of Section~\ref{subsec::single_triangular_decomposition} can be achieved by considering cliques of size greater than three in order to categorize tree structure whereby an internal vertex is connected to more than one leaf. Whilst finding a clique of size $\geq k$ for fixed $k$ is polynomial, with complexity of order $O(n^k)$ by simply checking all tuples of size $k$, the decision problem of whether a graph $G$ contains a clique of size $k$, as well as the problem of finding a largest clique, is $\mathbf{NP}$-complete. Thus, it is necessary to consider only cliques of fixed size $4$, for which the problem of determining their existence has polynomial complexity $O(n^4)$. This however is sufficient for classifying any \hyperref[def::pendant_fork]{pendant fork}, since any branching vertex with exactly two leaves will generate a $K_4$ in the square, whilst a branching vertex with more than two leaves will generate multiple $K_4$s of which one can be chosen by arbitrarily selecting any two leaves.

Consider vertices $s_1,s_2,u,v\in V$ such that $K_4 = \{s_1,s_2,u,v\}$ induces a $K_4$ in $G$ and for each leaf $s_1, s_2$ of $u$ their neighbourhoods are equivalent and give the common neighbourhood $N_L = N_G(s_1)\setminus K_4 =N_G(s_2) \setminus K_4$. If
\begin{equation} \label{eq::tetrahedron_neighbourhood_assignment}
\big( N_G(s_1)\setminus K_4 \big) = \big( N_G(s_2)\setminus K_4 \big) \subset \big( N_G(u)\setminus K_4 \big) \subseteq \big( N_G(v)\setminus K_4 \big),
\end{equation}
and if every vertex in the common neighbourhood of each leaf has no neighbours outside the tetrahedron and that neighbourhood itself,
\begin{equation} \label{eq::tetrahedron_leaf_class}
N_G(w) \subseteq K_4 \cup N_L \quad \forall w\in N_L,
\end{equation}
then $u$ is designated as the internal vertex, $v$ as the external vertex, and $s_1,s_2$ as the leaf vertices of the tetrahedron.

Under Equations~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_neighbourhood_assignment} and~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_leaf_class}, the vertices $s_1$ and $s_2$ correspond to leaves adjacent to a common internal vertex $u$, whilst $v$ acts as the connecting vertex between $u$ and the remainder of the graph. The vertices with identical minimal neighbourhood structure correspond to leaves adjacent to a common internal vertex $u$, whilst the vertex $v$, having maximal neighbourhood containment, acts as the connecting vertex between $u$ and the remainder of the graph.

For $n=4$, the vertex $u$ is a binary branching vertex contained in a unique $K_4$. In particular, when $n=5$, the branching vertex $u$ has three leaf children $s_1,s_2,s_3$, producing $\displaystyle {3 \choose 2} =3$ distinct $K_4$s
\[
\{s_1,s_2,u,v\},\quad \{s_1,s_3,u,v\},\quad \{s_2,s_3,u,v\}.
\]

More generally, suppose $u$ has $k$ leaf children $s_1,\dots,s_k$, and that these vertices together with $v$ induce a $K_{k+2}$. Then every pair of leaves $(s_i,s_j)$ determines a distinct $K_4$ $\{s_i,s_j,u,v\}$ where the total number of such $K_4$s generated by this branching configuration is $ \displaystyle {k \choose 2}$. Suppose that $s_1,s_2$ are chosen without loss of generality, then if the $K_4$ satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_neighbourhood_assignment} and Equation~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_leaf_class} have
\begin{equation} \label{eq::tetrahedron_fix}
  x_{s_1u}=x_{us_1} = x_{s_2u}=x_{us_2} = x_{uv} = x_{vu} = 1. 
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Tetrahedron Decomposition Interchangeability] \label{prop::textrahedron_decomposition_equality}
  Let $s_1,s_2,u,v$ form a $K_4$ in square $G$. If Equation~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_neighbourhood_assignment} and Equation~\eqref{eq::tetrahedron_leaf_class} are satisfied and
  \[
  N_G(u)\setminus K_4=N_G(v)\setminus K_4,
  \]
  then one can choose to assign
  \[
  x_{s_1u}=x_{us_1}=x_{s_2u}=x_{us_2}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1
  \]
  in the tree root $H$ without loss of generality.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Since $s_1,s_2,u,v$ form a $K_4$ in $G$ and
  \[
  N_G(u)\setminus K_4=N_G(v)\setminus K_4,
  \]
  the vertices $u$ and $v$ have identical neighbourhoods outside $K_4$. Moreover, since $u$ and $v$ are both adjacent to every vertex of $K_4$, it follows that
  \[
  N_G(u)=N_G(v).
  \]
  Thus, exchanging $u$ and $v$ is an automorphism of $G$. Hence, if a tree root $H$ has $v$ as the internal vertex of the tetrahedral configuration, applying this automorphism allows the roles of $u$ and $v$ to be exchanged without changing the square graph. Therefore, without loss of generality may assume that $u$ is the internal vertex of the tetrahedral configuration and assign
  \[
  x_{s_1u}=x_{us_1}=x_{s_2u}=x_{us_2}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1.
  \]
\end{proof}

\section{Unicliqual Vertex Linear Program} \label{sec::unicliqual_vertex_linear_program}


The key idea is to strengthen the formulation by adding constraints corresponding to edges that are known to occur in a graph root whenever a root exists. Using, Lemma~\eqref{lemma::tree_pendant_pendant_fork}, the formulations developed in Subsection~\ref{subsec::single_triangular_decomposition}--\ref{subsec::tetrahedron_decomposition} can be combined into one succinct linear program by first identifying a leaf-steam structure involving three vertices $s, u, v$. In particular, if a graph root exists, the structural properties of the graph guarantee that there is a root containing the identified edges. We may therefore safely fix the corresponding variables to $1$, thereby restricting the formulation to solutions containing these edges without excluding any graph root.

If $s$ is a leaf, $u$ is its corresponding stem, and there exists $v\in N_G[u]\setminus\{u\}$ such that
\[
N_G[s]\subset N_G[u]\subseteq N_G[v],
\]
with all vertices in $N_G[s]\setminus\{u,v\}$ having the same closed neighbourhood as $s$, then the corresponding root edges can be fixed by imposing
\begin{equation} \label{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix}
  x_{su}=x_{us}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Unicliqual Vertex Interchangeability] \label{prop::unicliqual_decomposition_equality}
  Let $s,u,v$ form a $K_3$ in square $G$, where $s$ is unicliqual. If $N_G[u]=N_G[v]$ then $u$ and $v$ may be chosen arbitrarily as the corresponding vertex in the tree root $H$ without loss of generality.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Since $s$ is unicliqual, the maximal clique containing $s$ is uniquely determined. If this clique satisfies the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop::single_triangular_decomposition_equality}, then that proposition permits either $u$ or $v$ to be chosen as the corresponding vertex, since $N_G[u]=N_G[v]$ gives $\deg_G(u)=\deg_G(v)$. If the configuration instead satisfies the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop::textrahedron_decomposition_equality}, then the equality $N_G[u]=N_G[v]$ gives
  \[
  N_G(u)\setminus K_4=N_G(v)\setminus K_4,
  \]
  so either $u$ or $v$ may be chosen in the tetrahedron fixing. Hence, in either case, $u$ and $v$ are interchangeable, and either may be chosen without loss of generality.
\end{proof}

It remains to verify that whenever a root $H$ of $G$ exists, the addition of the constraint Equation~\eqref{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix} preserves the feasibility of the linear program.

\begin{proposition}[Validity of Unicliqual Vertex Fix] \label{prop::unicliqual_vertex_fix_feasibility}
  If a root $H$ of $G$ exists, then $\max\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$ is feasible.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose that a root $H$ of $G$ exists. By Proposition~\ref{prop::linear_relaxation_feasibility}, $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is feasible. It remains to show that there exists a root whose adjacency matrix satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix}.

  Let $s,u,v$ be the vertices identified by the unicliqual vertex fixing. By Proposition~\ref{prop::unicliqual_decomposition_equality}, if $s,u,v$ form the corresponding $K_3$ and $N_G[u]=N_G[v]$, then $u$ and $v$ may be chosen arbitrarily as the corresponding vertex in the tree root without loss of generality. Thus, if the given root $H$ does not satisfy Equation~\eqref{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix}, the roles of $u$ and $v$ may be interchanged to obtain another root $H'$ of $G$ for which
  \[
  x_{su}=x_{us}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1.
  \]
  Hence the adjacency matrix of $H'$ is a feasible solution of $\mathcal{IP}(G)$ that satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix}. Since every feasible solution of $\mathcal{IP}(G)$ is feasible for $\mathcal{LP}(G)$, this solution is also feasible for $\max\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$. Therefore, $\max\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$ is feasible whenever a root $H$ of $G$ exists.
\end{proof}

\section{Tree Proof}

We first show that the unicliqual vertex linear program will always fix a sparse pendant or pendant fork. 

\begin{lemma} \label{lemma::unicliqual_vertex_fix}
  Let $G$ and $T$ be graphs such that $G = T^2$ and $T$ is a tree. $\max\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$ always fixes a sparse pendant or pendant fork subgraph of $T$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  By Lemma~\ref{lemma::tree_pendant_pendant_fork}, $T$ either contains a sparse pendant or a pendant fork subgraph. Let $s$ be a leaf of this subgraph and let $u$ be its stem. Since $s$ is a leaf of $T$, every vertex of  $N_G[s]$ is either $s$, $u$, the neighbours of $u$ in $T$, or a vertex at distance two from $s$ through $u$. Hence
  \[
  N_G[s] \subset N_G[u].
  \]
  Moreover, if $v$ is the neighbour of $u$ in $T$ that connects the sparse pendant or pendant fork to the remainder of $T$, then every vertex adjacent to $u$ in $T$ is also within distance two of $v$ in $T$.
  This gives 
  \[
  N_G[u] \subseteq N_G[v].
  \]
  In the pendant fork case, the other leaf $s'$ of the fork satisfies $N_G[s']=N_G[s]$, since both $s$ and $s'$ are leaves adjacent to the same vertex $u$. Thus the vertices in $N_G[s]\setminus\{u,v\}$ have the same closed neighbourhood as $s$.

  Therefore the vertices $s,u,v$ satisfy the conditions for  Equation~\eqref{eq::unicliqual_vertex_fix}, which imposes
  \[
  x_{su}=x_{us}=x_{uv}=x_{vu}=1.
  \]
\end{proof}

\chapter{Further Work}

In this chapter, we discuss additional work, further concepts, and interesting ideas arising from structural properties that the square graph intrinsically possesses. 

Previous methods for solving specific classes of roots have been developed by augmenting the linear programming framework with additional constraints and objective functions. Nevertheless, integrating known polynomial-time solutions for trees into an overall framework, while maintaining the internal consistency that valid square graphs must always correspond to feasible instances, does not appear to justify the use of a comparatively complex polynomial-time framework such as the interior-point method. 

It is, however, possible to construct stronger exact algorithms by performing a polynomial number of runs of the linear program. Each run can be used to identify additional structural information, fix variables, or further restrict the possible solutions before solving the resulting instance again. This may provide a useful mechanism through which improvements to the base linear program can have a much greater effect than the improvement obtained from a single LP solution alone. In particular, a stronger base program provides more integral variables and structural information, which can then be propagated through subsequent runs of the LP relaxation.

The following sections illustrate several directions in which this can be explored. 

\section{Vertex Deletion} \label{sec::vertex_deletion}

We can use an LP to find the set of all graphs on $n \leq 5$ vertices that admit a root. From Table~\eqref{tab::unicliqual_vertex_linear_program_outcomes} all square graphs can be completely categorized for small $n$, such that, whenever a root $H$ exists, an integral solution is obtained, while whenever no root $H$ exists, the linear program is infeasible. 

In particular, consider arbitrarily removing a vertex from $G$ and applying the same linear program to the resulting subgraph. If the subgraph has an integral solution, this provides structural information about a possible root on the reduced vertex set. We may then fix a partial solution using vertices whose root edges are not generated by a $P_2$ whose middle vertex is the removed vertex. The natural question is whether this information can subsequently be used to constrain the original graph when the deleted vertex is reintroduced.

More specifically, if deleting a vertex produces a graph for which the linear program has an integral solution, it is worth investigating whether the corresponding solution can be extended to the original graph and, if so, whether the possible extensions can be restricted to a small number of cases. If such extensions can be characterized, vertex deletion provides a mechanism through which information obtained from smaller instances can be propagated back to the original graph.

Let $G^-$ be the subgraph of $G$ defined by $\displaystyle V^- = V - {v}, E_{G^-} =  \{ E_G \backslash {uv \mid u \in V^-} \}$. If $G^-$ admits a valid square root, denote it by $H^-$. Let $H$ be a square root of $G$ satisfying $H^2=G$, and let $H_-$ be the subgraph of $H$ obtained by deleting $v$, defined by $\displaystyle V^-, \{ E_H \backslash {uv \mid u \in V^-}\}$. 

In general, $H_-$ need not be a square root of $G^-$, since the deletion of $v$ from $H$ may remove copies of $P_2$ between vertices in $V^-$ which results in $\left(H_-\right)^2\neq G^-$. Nevertheless, an integral solution of the LP for $G^-$ provides a candidate set of root edges on $V^-$ which can be used to refine the LP for $G$. 

\begin{algorithm} \caption{Vertex-Deletion Linear Program} \label{algo:vertex_deletion}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} A graph $G$
    \State \textbf{Output:} A LP model $(m,x)$
    \State Construct and solve $\mathcal{LP}(G)$.
    \If{$\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is integral}
        \State \Return $(m,x)$
    \EndIf
    \State Set $ \displaystyle F=\left\{\{a,b\}\in\binom{V_G}{2}:x_{ab}=x_{ba}=1\right\}$.
    \State Compute $ \displaystyle d_F(u)=\left|\left\{w\in N_G(u):\{u,w\}\notin F\right\}\right|$ for each $u\in V_G$.
    \State Choose $v\in\arg\min_{u\in V_G}d_F(u)$ and set $V^-=V_G \setminus\{v\}$.
    \State Construct $G^-=G[V^-]$ and inherit the fixing $ \displaystyle 
    F^-=\left\{\{a,b\}\in F:a,b\in V^-\right\}$.
    \State Construct and solve $\mathcal{LP}(G^-)+F^-$.
    \If{$\mathcal{LP}(G^-)+F^-$ is integral}
        \State Construct $H^-$ from its integral solution.
    \Else
        \State \Return $(m,x)$
    \EndIf
    \State Construct a new copy of $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ and impose $x_{ab}=x_{ba}=1$ for every $\{a,b\}\in F$ and every $ab\in E_{H^-}$.
    \State Solve the modified LP.
    \If{the modified LP is infeasible}
        \State \Return $(m,x)$
    \Else
        \State \Return the modified LP solution $(m,x)$
    \EndIf
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The recursive algorithm works as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
  \item Solve $\mathcal{LP}(G)$. If the LP is infeasible, the procedure terminates. If the LP is integral, the corresponding graph is retained as a candidate square root and the procedure terminates. Otherwise, let $\displaystyle F\subseteq\binom{V}{2}$ denote the set of unordered vertex pairs for which the solution satisfies $x_{ab}=x_{ba}=1$.
  \item For each $u\in V$, define its unfixed degree with respect to $F$ to be $d_F(u)=\{w \in N_G(u):{u,w} \notin F\}$. Choose a vertex $\displaystyle v \in \arg\min_{u \in V} \{d_F(u)\}$ and construct $G^-=G-v$ by deleting $v$ and all edges incident to $v$. Let $V^-=V\setminus{v}$ and let
  $F^-={{a,b}\in F,b\in V^-}$ denote the fixing inherited by $G^-$.
  \item Solve $\mathcal{LP}(G^-) + F^-$, which is $G^-$ with the additional fixing $F^-$.
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item If $\mathcal{LP}(G^-) + F^-$ is fractional, let $H^-$ denote the corresponding root of $F^-$.
      \item If $\mathcal{LP}(G^-) + F^-$ is infeasible, the candidate $H^-$ is discarded. 
      \item If $\mathcal{LP}(G^-) + F^-$ is fractional, recursively apply the vertex deletion algorithm to solve $\mathcal{G^-}$. If the recursive deletion produces an integral solution, let $H^-$ denote the corresponding root of $G^-$.  
    \end{enumerate}
  \item If an integral candidate $H^-$ has been obtained, construct $\mathcal{LP}(G\mid F,H^-)$ by taking $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ and imposing $x_{ab}=x_{ba}=1$ for every ${a,b}\in F$ and every pair $ \displaystyle {a,b}\in\binom{V^-}{2}$ such that $ab\in E_{H^-}$. No additional constraint is imposed on pairs for which $ab\notin E_{H^-}$, and the variables corresponding to pairs involving $v$ remain unrestricted apart from the fixing induced by $F$.
  \item Solve $\mathcal{LP}(G\mid F,H^-)$.
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item If $\mathcal{LP}(G\mid F,H^-)$ is feasible, the fixing obtained from $H^-$ is consistent with the LP formulation for $G$, and the resulting solution is retained.
      \item If $\mathcal{LP}(G\mid F,H^-)$ is infeasible, the candidate $H^-$ is discarded. This does not imply that $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is infeasible; it only shows that the candidate root structure $H^-$ is incompatible with the current fixing for $G$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \item \label{step:no_integral_hminus} If no integral candidate $H^-$ is obtained from $\mathcal{LP}(G^-)+F^-$ or from the recursive application to $G^-$, then no further fixing is obtained from the vertex deletion step. The current feasible solution of $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is retained, and the procedure terminates.
  \item If an integral candidate $H^-$ is obtained but $\mathcal{LP}(G\mid F,H^-)$ is infeasible, the fixing induced by $H^-$ is discarded. The current feasible solution of $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is retained, and the procedure terminates. This does not imply that $\mathcal{LP}(G)$ is infeasible; it only shows that the candidate root structure $H^-$ is incompatible with the current fixing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Symbolic Sets} \label{sec::symbolic_sets}

Lemma~\ref{lemma::g_ab=0,two_edge_sum} gives structure into the feasible solution of the LP. Using the maximization objective function Lemma~\ref{lemma::g_ab=0,two_edge_sum}, if $g_{ij}=g_{jk}=1$$G$ but $g_{ki}=0$, $x_{ij} + x_{jk} \leq 1$. 

If one could determine when this holds with equality $x_{ij} + x_{jk} = 1$, this would enable $x_{ij}, x_{jk}$ to be easily categorized since if one is $0$ then the other is necessarily $1$. In particular, this may be useful in resolving scenarios where the maximum fractional objective function is greater than the optimal integer objective function corresponding to a valid square root.

  \[
  x_{av} + x_{vb} = 1 \text{ or }  x_{av} + x_{vb} = 0. 
  \]
\end{conjecture}

In this example, both $\{(1,4), (4,0), (4,2), (4,3) \}$ and $\{(1,5), (3,5), (5,0), (5,2)\}$ are consistent sets. 

To formalise this two-colouring structure, we define an auxiliary graph that represents the relationships between edges of $G$. Let $\operatorname{Aux}(G)$ be the graph whose vertices correspond to the edges of $G$. Two vertices of $\operatorname{Aux}(G)$ are adjacent whenever their corresponding edges in $G$ form a $P_2$ for which the $=1$ case of Conjecture~\eqref{conjecture::two_edges_linear_maximization} holds. Thus, if the corresponding edges are $av$ and $vb$, then
\[
x_{av}+x_{vb}=1.
\]
Consequently, if $x_{av}=\alpha$ then $x_{vb}=1-\alpha$, and adjacent vertices of $\operatorname{Aux}(G)$ must receive different colours.

For the graph in Figure~\ref{fig::130_6}, the auxiliary graph contains three disconnected bipartite components, corresponding to the three consistent sets. The two colour classes within each component represent the complementary values associated with a separate parameter $\alpha$, $\beta$, or $\gamma$. Thus, the edge variables in the first consistent set are assigned $\alpha$ or $1-\alpha$, those in the second are assigned $\beta$ or $1-\beta$, and those in the third are assigned $\gamma$ or $1-\gamma$. Since the components are disconnected, these three assignments are independent.

\begin{definition}[Consistent Set] \label{def::consistent_set}
  A \textbf{consistent set} $\mathcal{CS}(G)\subseteq E_G$ is a maximal set containing at least three edges whose corresponding vertices induce a connected bipartite subgraph of $\operatorname{Aux}(G)$.
\end{definition}

Consider Figure~\ref{fig::115_6} where assigning $\alpha = x_{03}$ gives using Conjecture~\eqref{conjecture::two_edges_linear_maximization} that $x_{31} = 1 - \alpha$ since $g_{01} = 0$. Likewise as $g_{12} = 0$ have $x_{32} = \alpha$ and $x_{03} + x_{32} \leq 1$ such that $\displaystyle \alpha \leq \frac{1}{2}$. By symmetry this likewise holds for $\beta = x_{05} = x_{15}$, $1-\beta = x_{52}$ and $\gamma = x_{42} = x_{41}$, $1-\gamma = x_{40}$ giving $\displaystyle \beta, \gamma \leq \frac{1}{2}$. 

Maximizing the objective function thus gives $\displaystyle \alpha=\beta=\gamma=\frac{1}{2}$ which is a fractional solution. It necessitates the arbitrarily fixing of edges to give a valid integral solution. Setting $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0$ with $x_{31}=x_{52}=x_{40} = 1$ and the remaining to $0$ gives the solution Figure~\ref{fig::115_6_root}, yet this is not doable in general. In this example, all of $\{(0,3), (3,1), (3,2) \}$, $\{(0,5), (1,5), (5,2)\}$ and $\{(4,2), (4,1), (4,0)\}$ are inconsistent sets. 

\begin{definition}[Inconsistent Set] \label{def::inconsistent_set}
  Let an \textbf{inconsistent set} $\mathcal{IS}(G) \subseteq E_G$ be a set with at least three edges such that every pair of edges in $\mathcal{IS}(G)$ satisfies the equality with value $1$ in Conjecture~\eqref{conjecture::two_edges_linear_maximization}, but does not admit a valid $2$-colouring. That is, there is no assignment of $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$ to the edges of $\mathcal{IS}(G)$ that simultaneously satisfies all equalities in the set. In particular, if a pair of edges is required to have the same value, then the equality $\alpha + \alpha \leq 1$ implies $\displaystyle 0 \leq \alpha \leq \frac{1}{2}$ or $1 - \alpha + 1 - \alpha \leq 1$ such that $\displaystyle 1 \geq \alpha \geq \frac{1}{2}$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{conjecture} \label{conjecture::consistent_set_conjecture}
  Let $G$ be a square graph whose consistent sets have a union forming a subgraph of $G$. Then the symbols associated with each consistent set can be fixed to values in $\{0,1\}$ whilst preserving feasibility of the LP.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{conjecture} \label{conjecture::inconsistent_set_conjecture}
Let $G$ be a square graph with at least one inconsistent set. Then at least one inconsistent set contains a star.
\end{conjecture}

Using Conjectures~\ref{conjecture::consistent_set_conjecture}--\ref{conjecture::inconsistent_set_conjecture}, we can formulate an edge enforcement algorithm that first identifies independent consistent sets and fixes their edges by setting the symbol to be $0$ or $1$. Before then identifying stars within the remaining inconsistent sets and fixing an edge within them. 

Suppose that the edge enforcement procedure produces $k$ consistent sets. An exhaustive procedure may need to consider up to $2^k$ different assignments and since $k$ may depend on $n$, this is not polynomial in general. In particular, to obtain a polynomial bound from exhaustive enumeration, it would be necessary to prove that $
k=O(\log n)$, since then
\[
2^k=2^{O(\log n)}=n^{O(1)}.
\]
Hence, instead of exploring the full set, the greedy algorithm explores at most two LP feasibility tests for the selected group before moving to the next group and therefore avoids the exponential branching over combinations of consistent sets. 

\begin{algorithm} \caption{Greedy edge enforcement}
\label{algorithm::greedy_edge_enforcement}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} A graph $G$
    \State \textbf{Output:} A LP model $(m,x)$
    \State Construct and solve $\mathcal{LP}(G)$.
    \State Identify the consistent sets and inconsistent sets.
    \State Mark all sets as unfixed.
    \While{the current LP solution is fractional}
      \If{there is an unfixed consistent set}
          \State Select a consistent set $C$.
          \State Let $a$ be the symbol associated with $C$.
          \State Add the constraint $a=0$ and solve the LP.
          \If{the resulting LP is feasible}
              \State Keep the constraint $a=0$.
          \Else
              \State Remove the constraint $a=0$.
              \State Add the constraint $a=1$ and solve the LP.

              \If{the resulting LP is feasible}
                  \State Keep the constraint $a=1$.
              \Else
                  \State Remove the constraint $a=1$.
              \EndIf
          \EndIf
          \State Mark $C$ as fixed.
      \ElsIf{there is an unfixed inconsistent set}

          \State Select an inconsistent set and identify a star
          \State with centre $v$.
          \State Order the edges of the star according to their
          \State current LP values, considering the most integral edge first.

          \For{each edge $e$ of the selected star}
              \State Add the constraint $x_e=1$ and solve the LP.

              \If{the resulting LP is feasible}
                  \State Keep the constraint $x_e=1$.
                  \State Mark the inconsistent set as fixed.
                  \State \textbf{break}.
              \Else
                  \State Remove the constraint $x_e=1$.
              \EndIf
          \EndFor
      \Else
          \State \textbf{break}.
      \EndIf
    \EndWhile
    \State \Return the resulting LP model.
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Duality} \label{sec::duality}

One of the strengths of a linear program in solving problems is the ability to leverage duality. In particular, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions give deeper insight into the geometric interpretation of the feasible region through the introduction of dual variables. If the dual variable is non-zero, then the corresponding primal constraint is tight, whilst if the dual variable is zero, then the primal constraint is not satisfied. This enables solutions to the linear program to be viewed directly in the geometric region. 


Importantly, the relationship between the primal and dual is highlighted by their solution to the objective function. For a linear program, these conditions reduce to primal feasibility, dual feasibility, and complementary slackness. In particular, when both the primal and dual are feasible, strong duality implies that their optimal objective values are equal.

\section{Rounding} \label{sec::rounding}

Finally, we consider a simple heuristic for further refining fractional LP solutions - rounding. Since each edge variable takes a value in $[0,1]$,  many strategies are possible, ranging from probabilistic rounding to systematically fixing each fractional edge in both possible ways. Because every edge variable must ultimately take a value in $\{0,1\}$ in an integral solution, a more exhaustive approach is to branch on each fractional variable, assigning it one of its two possible integral values. This produces a binary search tree whose depth is bounded by the number of fractional variables.

If the number of fractional variables is bounded by $O(\log n)$, where $n=|V_G|$, then the search tree has at most $2^{O(\log n)}=n^{O(1)}$ leaves. Therefore, provided that the work performed at each node of the search tree is polynomial in $n$, the overall algorithm runs in polynomial time. Classical techniques such as pruning, bounding, and depth-first or breadth-first search may then be leveraged to evaluate all end nodes efficiently: pruning discards branches whose LP bound exceeds the incumbent, bounding limits the depth of exploration, and the search order determines how quickly an integral solution is found. 

In Algorithm~\ref{algo::greedy_rounding}, we use only a simple greedy rounding procedure. At each iteration, the current fractional LP solution is examined and a single fractional edge is selected. The selection is based on the distance of its value from $0.5$, so that fractional values closer to $0$ or $1$ are considered first. The selected edge is then fixed to $0$ if its value is below $0.5$ and to $1$ otherwise. The LP is subsequently resolved with all previous fixings retained.

If the resulting LP is infeasible, the rounding procedure terminates and the previous feasible fractional solution is retained. If the resulting LP remains fractional, another fractional edge is selected and the process continues. The procedure terminates when an integral solution is obtained or when no further feasible fixing can be made.

\begin{algorithm} \caption{Greedy LP Rounding} \label{algo::greedy_rounding}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} A graph $G$
    \State \textbf{Output:} A LP model $(m,x)$

    \vspace{0.5em}

    \State Initialize $F=\emptyset$ and construct $\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$.
    \State Solve $\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$.
    \While{the current LP solution is fractional}
      \State For each fractional edge $ab$, compute its rounding score $ s_{ab}=|x_{ab}-0.5|$.
      \State Select a fractional edge $ab$ with maximum score.
      \State Set
      \[
      \widehat{x}_{ab}=\widehat{x}_{ba}=
      \begin{cases}
      0, & x_{ab} < 0.5,\\
      1, & x_{ab} \geq 0.5.
      \end{cases}
      \]
      \State Add $ab$ with its assigned value to $F$.
      \State Reconstruct $\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$ with all fixings in $F$.
      \State Solve $\mathcal{UVLP}(G) + F$.
      \If{$\mathcal{UVLP}(G) + F$ is infeasible}
          \State \textbf{return} the previous feasible solution $(m,x)$.
      \EndIf
      \If{$\mathcal{UVLP}(G) + F$ is integral}
          \State \textbf{return} the integral solution $(m,x)$.
      \EndIf
    \EndWhile
    \State \textbf{return} $(m,x)$.
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The procedure is greedy because only one fixing is selected at each iteration and no alternative fixing is explored. Thus, unlike exhaustive rounding or branch-and-bound approaches, it does not construct a binary search tree of possible fixings. Since there are at most $\binom{|V_G|}{2}$ edge variables to fix and each iteration requires one LP solve, the rounding procedure requires at most $O(n^2)$ LP solves. Consequently, as $\mathcal{UVLP}(G)$ can be solved in polynomial time, the greedy rounding procedure is polynomial-time.

The choice of the base linear program is important for the effectiveness of the subsequent rounding procedure. The rounding procedure begins with a fractional solution and iteratively fixes selected edge variables. Consequently, the constraints imposed by the base formulation determine which fractional solutions can be successfully rounded and which become infeasible during the fixing process.

The outcomes obtained by using the maximum-objective linear program and the unicliqual vertex linear program as base formulations are shown in Table~\ref{tab::max_rounding_program_outcomes} and Table~\ref{tab::unicliqual_rounding_program_outcomes}. These results demonstrate that the choice of base formulation has a substantial effect on the effectiveness of the subsequent rounding procedure. Although $\mathcal{UVLP}$ produces a smaller increase in successfully rounded solutions, the improvement remains significant, as the additional structural constraints enable more root instances to be correctly classified.
